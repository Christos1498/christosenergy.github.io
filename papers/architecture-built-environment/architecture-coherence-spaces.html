For a long time I thought identity was something people chose. That assumption shaped the way I listened to people and the way I judged them. Over time something stopped adding up. I kept watching people change without realizing they were changing. I watched thoughtful people become rigid in certain environments. I watched generous people become defensive. Geometry is not neutral. Straight lines, hard corners, uniform repetition, and abrupt scale shifts keep the brain in edge detection mode. Geometry is the hidden curriculum.— Joshua Farrior, from "The Separation Was Never Personal: Identity, Environment, and the Architecture of Belief"
Abstract

This paper proposes that architecture is best understood not as the design of buildings but as the design of coherence fields that occupants inhabit continuously, extending Joshua Farrior's personal essay quoted above with a proposed theoretical mechanism. It draws on genuine, established architectural acoustics, real environmental psychology research on the built environment's effect on healing and social behavior, and real chronobiology, and extends these with the framework's own proposed coherence-field mechanism and a "positional identity" hypothesis.

Three device and assessment concepts are proposed: a Building Coherence Assessment, a design protocol, and a reference temple design. All framework-specific claims beyond the cited real research, including the coherence-field mechanism and the positional identity typology, are presented as this paper's own hypothesis, pending the validation proposed in Part VIII, not as an established finding in architecture, psychology, or physics.

I. What Buildings Do to Their Inhabitants

The paper's starting premise, that the built environment measurably affects physiology and behavior, has real support: chronic environmental stressors (noise, poor lighting, crowding) are genuinely associated with elevated cortisol and reduced HRV in the stress physiology literature, and real research has documented worse outcomes in poorly designed institutional spaces (addressed with a specific real citation in Part VI below). The paper's added hypothesis proposes a specific mechanism for this, a "coherence field" that buildings generate and occupants continuously couple to via biophotonic and electromagnetic pathways described elsewhere in this library. This specific field mechanism is the paper's own hypothesis, not an established explanation for the real environmental-psychology findings it draws on.

II. On the Positional Identity Framework

The paper's most far-reaching proposal holds that identity is "positional" rather than fixed, that a person's behavioral flexibility, empathy, and capacity to tolerate ambiguity shift substantially with their environment, and it proposes three named identity modes ("Cubical," "Tetrahedral," "Octahedral") mapped to specific geometric and social conditions. The underlying premise that environment shapes psychological state has genuine support: chronic stress, crowding, and sensory environments are documented in real psychology and neuroscience research to affect cognitive flexibility and threat perception. The specific three-mode typology mapped to platonic solid geometry is this paper's own proposed framework, not an established model in psychology, and should be read as the author's own interpretive lens, offered in the spirit of reducing contempt in social conflict, rather than as a validated diagnostic or personality framework.

III. Sacred Architecture

The paper's observation that sacred building traditions across unconnected cultures converged on broadly similar features, generous proportion systems, specific reverberant acoustics, natural materials, solar and seasonal alignment, and water features, is a genuine and often-discussed observation in architectural history, though the explanation for that convergence (shared engineering constraints, cultural exchange, or the paper's own proposed coherence-physics explanation) remains an open and interesting question rather than a settled one. The paper's proposed physical mechanism, that these traditions were specifically solving a coherence-field optimization problem, is its own hypothesis, offered as one possible explanation among others historians and archaeologists have proposed.

IV. Acoustic Coherence

Reverberation time (RT60), the time for sound to decay by 60 decibels in a space, is a real, standard, and important measurement in architectural acoustics, and real acoustic engineering standards do specify different target RT60 ranges for different space types (classrooms, performance halls, worship spaces) based on genuine research into speech intelligibility and listening comfort. The paper's proposed specific RT60 targets for "coherence amplification" in healing, ceremonial, and meditation spaces are the paper's own proposed values within this real measurement framework, not independently validated against established acoustic design standards, and are not reproduced at exact value in this public version.

V. Materials, Light, and Electromagnetic Environment

Circadian lighting effects are genuinely well-established chronobiology: blue-spectrum light suppresses melatonin production, and the mismatch between modern artificial lighting and natural light's full spectrum has real documented effects on sleep and circadian regulation. "Sick building syndrome," a real, WHO-recognized cluster of symptoms associated with certain indoor environments, and the general premise that ventilation, lighting, and material off-gassing affect occupant wellbeing, are genuine and established in building science and occupational health.

The paper's proposed material "coherence class" ratings, assigning specific tiers to granite, wood, concrete, aluminum, and other building materials based on proposed piezoelectric and electromagnetic properties, are the paper's own classification system, not an established material science standard, though the underlying premise that some building materials are more electromagnetically reflective or reactive than others (relevant to real EMF shielding practice) is grounded in genuine physics. The specific proposed coherence-class assignments and their claimed physiological effects are not established findings.

VI. Where Modern Buildings Fall Short

Two specific claims in this section connect to genuine, well-known published research the source material describes without naming. The claim that open-plan offices reduce face-to-face interaction rather than increasing it, contrary to their stated design rationale, matches a real, widely cited study: Bernstein and Turban's (2018) research published in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B found that open-plan offices measurably reduced face-to-face interaction and increased electronic communication. The claim that hospital design affects healing outcomes, specifically that patients with more favorable surroundings recover better, matches Roger Ulrich's (1984) genuinely famous and influential study in Science, which found that surgical patients with a view of nature had shorter hospital stays and required less pain medication than those facing a brick wall. Both are real, independently verifiable findings.

The paper's proposed explanatory mechanism for both, that these outcomes result specifically from coherence-field effects (geometry, acoustics, material class, and EMF density acting through the framework's proposed field physics), is this paper's own hypothesis. The cited real research documents the outcomes; it does not establish this specific proposed mechanism as their cause, and other explanations (attention restoration, stress reduction, reduced sensory overload, and privacy) are also actively discussed in the real environmental psychology literature.

VII. Proposed Assessment and Design Tools

The paper proposes a Building Coherence Assessment combining geometric, acoustic, material, lighting, electromagnetic, and earth-connection sub-scores into a single composite score; a corresponding design protocol intended as a supplement to standard architectural codes; and a reference temple design combining phi-ratio geometry, specific natural materials, and the device family described elsewhere in this library into a single proposed building. The complete scoring formula and its component weightings, the design protocol's specific numeric targets, and the reference design's detailed specifications are not disclosed in this public version. The source material's own estimated construction cost for the reference temple design falls in the low-to-mid seven-figure range; this is the paper's own cost estimate, not validated pricing.
