Abstract

The origin of cosmological perturbations — the primordial density fluctuations that seeded all structure in the universe — is one of the great successes of modern cosmology. Inflation stretches quantum vacuum fluctuations to macroscopic scales, producing the near-scale-invariant spectrum observed in the CMB. Yet inflation raises its own deep questions: What is the inflaton field? Why does it have its required potential? How do quantum fluctuations become classical density perturbations? This paper proposes a geometric interpretation: the primordial perturbations are not quantum vacuum fluctuations of an inflaton field — they are coherence fluctuations of the toroidal Christos-Saturnalia boundary in the early universe. Scale invariance emerges naturally from the self-similarity of the toroidal coherence field near a critical state. The slight red tilt reflects the slow evolution of the coherence field. The framework reproduces all successes of inflation without requiring an inflaton field, predicts a very small tensor-to-scalar ratio r < 10⁻³ (consistent with current limits), and predicts local non-Gaussianity f_NL ~ O(1) detectable by next-generation surveys.

2. Toroidal Coherence Fluctuations

2.1 The Early Universe as Toroidal Coherence Field

In the CTF, the early universe is a dynamic Christos-Saturnalia circulation near a coherence-critical state. The Christos current drives outward expansion; the Saturnalia return current provides the restoring force that prevents runaway expansion. Near the coherence critical point, the correlation length of the field diverges — producing a self-similar, nearly scale-invariant spectrum of fluctuations.

2.2 Scale Invariance from Coherence Criticality

At a coherence critical state, the two-point correlation function of coherence fluctuations becomes approximately scale-free. The corresponding power spectrum:

P_C(k) ∝ k⁻³ · (1 + η_C log(k/k_0))

is approximately scale-invariant with a small scale-dependent correction from the coherence field evolution rate η_C. The observed spectral tilt n_s − 1 = −0.035 corresponds to η_C ≈ 0.035 — the rate at which the coherence field evolves during the period when observable modes are generated. This measures how quickly the toroidal compactification scale changes during the period of maximum structure generation.

3. Predictions Distinguishing CTF from Inflation

3.1 Tensor-to-Scalar Ratio

In the CTF, tensor perturbations (gravitational waves) are suppressed because toroidal coherence fluctuations are intrinsically scalar. The CTF prediction:

r = P_t(k) / P_s(k) ~ (η_C)² ~ 10⁻³–10⁻⁴

substantially below the current limit of r < 0.036 (BICEP/Keck+Planck). Future experiments — LiteBIRD (targeting r ~ 0.001) and CMB-S4 — will definitively test this prediction. Detection of r > 10⁻² would distinguish standard large-field inflation from the CTF.

3.2 Oscillatory Features

P(k) = P_0(k) · [1 + β cos(2kL + ϕ)]   with β ~ 10⁻³–10⁻²

These oscillations are a unique CTF signature — not expected in simple single-field inflation without additional structure. Future surveys with k-resolution comparable to 1/L will test this prediction directly.

3.3 Non-Gaussianity

Standard single-field inflation predicts negligible non-Gaussianity (f_NL << 1). The CTF predicts local-type non-Gaussianity from the nonlinear coupling between coherence modes during the critical-state period:

f_NL^{local} ~ O(1)

within current Planck constraints (f_NL = −0.9 ± 5.1) but detectable by SPHEREx, Euclid, and the Roman Space Telescope, which target σ(f_NL) ~ 1.

4. The Quantum-to-Classical Transition

In inflation, the quantum-to-classical transition of fluctuations requires decoherence by environmental modes — a mechanism that is incomplete and contested. In the CTF, no such transition is needed. The coherence fluctuations of the Christos-Saturnalia boundary are classical field fluctuations from inception — they are not quantum vacuum fluctuations that must become classical. The CMB we observe is the classical imprint of classical coherence fluctuations. This eliminates one of the key conceptual difficulties of inflation without replacing it with new physics.

5. Relationship to Standard ΛCDM

The CTF changes only the mechanism generating the primordial spectrum — not the subsequent evolution. Standard ΛCDM transfer functions, recombination physics, acoustic oscillations, and late-time structure formation all proceed identically. The CTF spectrum P(k) differs from inflation only in the tensor amplitude, the oscillatory features, and the non-Gaussianity — all consistent with current observations and testable by next-generation surveys.

6. Falsifiable Predictions

  • r < 10⁻³: LiteBIRD and CMB-S4 should measure or bound r at this level within this decade. If r > 10⁻² is detected, the CTF tensor prediction is falsified.
  • Oscillatory power spectrum features at amplitude β ~ 10⁻³–10⁻²: Detectable in principle with DESI, Euclid, and Roman large-scale structure surveys.
  • f_NL ~ O(1): Detectable by SPHEREx. Null result f_NL < 0.1 would require re-examination of the nonlinear coupling strength.
  • No inflaton particle: Future colliders should continue to find no fundamental scalar fields beyond the Higgs.

7. Conclusion

The origin of cosmological perturbations is reinterpreted as coherence fluctuations of the toroidal early-universe field near a coherence-critical state. Scale invariance emerges from the self-similarity of the critical state. The spectral tilt is the coherence slow-roll rate. Tensor modes are suppressed because the coherence field is intrinsically scalar. The quantum-to-classical transition problem does not arise. The universe is not a quantum vacuum that inflated — it is a toroidal coherence field that breathed. The seeds of galaxies are the imprint of the torus.
