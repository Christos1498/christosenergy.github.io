Abstract

The cusp-core problem is a long-standing discrepancy between ΛCDM cosmological simulations and observations of dark matter-dominated dwarf galaxies. N-body simulations consistently predict dark matter halos with steep cuspy inner density profiles scaling as ρ(r) ∝ r⁻¹ (Navarro-Frenk-White profile). Observations of dwarf galaxies consistently prefer flat cored profiles with ρ(r) ≈ constant in the inner kiloparsec. This paper proposes a coherence-based mechanism: cusp flattening is a phase-mixing phenomenon driven by coherence decoherence in low-coherence dark matter orbits. The coherence-dependent inner slope α(r) = −1 · exp(−C(r)/C_core) continuously varies from cuspy in high-coherence halos to cored in low-coherence ones, naturally producing the observed diversity of inner slopes without invoking exotic physics.

1. The Problem

The Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile predicts:

ρ_NFW(r) = ρ₀ / [(r/r_s)(1 + r/r_s)²]

For r << r_s: ρ ∝ r⁻¹ — a steep cusp. Observations of dwarf galaxies consistently prefer cored profiles. The diversity is as problematic as the mean discrepancy: some dwarfs have cusps, some have cores. SIDM with a fixed cross-section predicts universal core formation; baryonic feedback models require different feedback efficiencies across similar-mass galaxies. Neither explains the diversity naturally.

2. Coherence Decoherence and Phase Mixing

2.1 Dark Matter Orbital Coherence

The CTF framework models each dark matter particle's orbital coherence as a function of orbital phase alignment, local density, tidal environment, and halo mass. Define a radial coherence profile:

C(r) = C₀ · exp(−r/r_C)

In massive cluster halos: C₀ > 0.7, r_C large. In isolated dwarf halos: C₀ ≈ 0.2–0.4, r_C small.

2.2 Phase Mixing and Core Formation

When C_orb falls below a critical threshold, orbital phases randomize over time. This decoherence produces velocity isotropization and density flattening. The equilibrium inner slope driven by coherence decoherence:

α(r) = −1 · exp(−C(r)/C_core)

For C(r) >> C_core: α ≈ −1 (cuspy). For C(r) << C_core: α ≈ 0 (cored). For intermediate C(r): continuous gradient between cusp and core — the diversity of observations.

This explains both the mean discrepancy (dwarf halos are low-coherence) and the diversity (coherence varies continuously with mass, environment, and orbital history). Dwarfs with cusps are explained by higher-than-average coherence from their formation environment or orbital history.

4. Core Size Scaling

The core radius r_c (where α = −0.5) scales with the coherence length:

r_c ∝ r_C ∝ 1/C₀

High-coherence halos: small r_C, small or no core. Low-coherence halos: large r_C, large core. This predicts: isolated dwarfs in voids (lowest coherence) should have the largest cores; cluster satellites (highest tidal disruption) should have tidally-induced cores growing over time.

5. Relationship to Baryonic Feedback

The CTF does not reject baryonic feedback. Supernova feedback can accelerate coherence decoherence by injecting energy into the dark matter potential, stirring orbits, and reducing C₀. The CTF prediction: coherence decoherence is a universal mechanism that operates even in dark matter-only simulations; baryonic feedback amplifies this process in star-forming systems. Dark matter-only simulations with coherence tracking should produce cores in low-C₀ halos without any baryonic feedback — a prediction that distinguishes CTF from pure feedback models.

6. Falsifiable Predictions

  • Core size r_c should anti-correlate with environmental coherence proxies (distance to nearest filament, tidal index, local density) across a statistical sample of dwarf galaxies. Testable with resolved rotation curves from LITTLE THINGS, SHIELD, and THINGS surveys.
  • Isolated void dwarfs should have the largest cores of any population — with α ≈ 0 in the inner kiloparsec. Directly testable with resolved H I rotation curves of galaxies identified in void catalogs.
  • Satellite dwarfs should be more cored than field dwarfs of the same mass — tidal decoherence amplifies core formation.
  • Dark matter-only simulations with coherence tracking should produce cores in low-mass halos without baryonic physics.

7. Conclusion

The cusp-core problem is a coherence decoherence phenomenon. Dark matter particles in low-coherence orbital configurations undergo phase mixing that isotropizes velocity distributions and flattens inner density profiles. The process is universal, geometric, and scale-dependent: massive halos maintain high coherence and retain cusps; low-mass dwarfs allow orbital decoherence and develop cores; the diversity of observations reflects the continuous variation of halo coherence with mass, environment, and orbital history.

α(r) = −1 · exp(−C(r)/C_core)   —   inner slope as coherence meter
