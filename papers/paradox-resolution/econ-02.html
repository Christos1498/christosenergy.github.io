Decision Coherence, Phase Overload, and the Coherence Limit of Discernment

Abstract

Barry Schwartz's paradox of choice: more options are not always better. Beyond a threshold, decision quality degrades, satisfaction decreases, and the probability of choosing any option at all falls. The jam study (Iyengar & Lepper 2000) showed 6 options produced 10× more purchases than 24 options. Standard explanations — opportunity cost salience, regret avoidance, decision fatigue — correctly identify contributing mechanisms without identifying the underlying structural limit. The structural limit is a coherence threshold: decision-making is a phase discrimination process, and every decision-maker has a finite phase discrimination capacity that bounds the number of options they can meaningfully evaluate simultaneously. Beyond this bound, options collapse into undifferentiated noise and the decision process fails.

2. Decision-Making as Phase Discrimination

In the CTF, each option in a decision set has a coherence signature. Decision-making is the process of identifying which option's coherence signature best phase-locks with the decision-maker's target state. Two options can be discriminated only if the decision-maker's current coherence C_decision satisfies:

Δϕ > C_decision / N_options   [minimum resolvable phase difference scales with option count]

As N_options increases, the minimum resolvable difference per option decreases, requiring higher C_decision to maintain discrimination. Beyond N_opt = C_decision / Δϕ_min, options become undiscriminable. The decision collapses.

An expert sommelier can discriminate 30 wines because domain-specific expertise has built fine-grained coherence configurations for wine — their Δϕ_min for wine options is very small. The same sommelier choosing between 30 cars experiences the same paradox as a novice.

3. Decision Fatigue as Coherence Depletion

Each act of discrimination uses coherence resources. Over many consecutive decisions, C_decision declines. As it declines, N_opt falls. Options that were discriminable early become indiscriminable later. This is why judges' parole decisions become less favorable through the morning (Danziger et al. 2011), why grocery shoppers make worse nutritional choices at the end of a shopping trip, and why medical residents make diagnostic errors more frequently at the end of a shift. Coherence depletion, not fatigue in the ordinary sense.

4. Satisficing as Coherence Conservation

Herbert Simon's satisficing (choose the first option meeting a threshold) is not a second-best approximation — it is a coherence-conservation strategy that may be optimal when choice sets are large and discrimination capacity is limited. By stopping at the first adequate option, the satisficer conserves coherence resources for subsequent decisions.

5. Design Implications and Falsifiable Predictions

The resolution is not "always provide fewer options." It is: match the number of options to the decision-maker's coherence capacity for that decision context. For unfamiliar domains: reduce options to C_decision-compatible number. For expert decision-makers in their domain: more options may genuinely improve outcomes.

  • N_opt scales with domain expertise: measured domain expertise should predict the option count at which choice quality peaks, linearly or with a power function, across diverse domains.
  • Intraday HRV predicts choice quality: HRV coherence before a decision task should predict choice quality — with the relationship steeper for larger option sets.
  • Meditation restores depleted discrimination capacity after deliberate coherence depletion (many consecutive decisions).

6. Conclusion

The paradox of choice is a coherence threshold phenomenon. Decision-making is phase discrimination. Phase discrimination has a finite capacity varying with domain expertise, current coherence state, and decision history. When option count exceeds discrimination capacity, the decision process fails — not because of psychological weakness, but because the coherence mathematics of discrimination set an upper bound.

N_opt = C_decision / Δϕ_min   —   the coherence mathematics of optimal choice

Resolution Framework — The Five Moves

Move AppliedApplication to This Paradox
Move 2: Coherence ThresholdN_opt is the coherence threshold for decision-making: the maximum options that can be meaningfully discriminated given current decision coherence and domain-specific capacity. Above N_opt: options collapse into undiscriminable noise; choice quality degrades. Below N_opt: options are genuinely discriminable. The paradox of choice is a threshold phenomenon: beneficial below the threshold, harmful above it.
Move 3: Correct ScaleThe paradox of choice appears to operate at the individual cognitive level. The actual mechanism operates at the coherence field level — the phase discrimination capacity of the decision-maker's field. Apply the correct scale and the right interventions become obvious: restore coherence capacity, not just reduce information.
