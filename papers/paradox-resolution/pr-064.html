Abstract

The too-big-to-fail (TBTF) problem is a specific tension within the small-scale structure crisis of ΛCDM cosmology. High-resolution dark matter simulations predict that the most massive subhalos orbiting the Milky Way — those with peak circular velocity V_max > 25–30 km/s — have gravitational potential wells deep enough to resist reionization suppression and should definitively host luminous dwarf galaxies. Yet the brightest observed Milky Way dwarf satellites are systematically less dense than these predicted subhalos. This paper proposes a coherence-based resolution: the TBTF subhalos exist at the predicted masses and densities, but their orbital coherence determines whether they cross the star formation threshold. Subhalos on high-coherence orbits (near-circular, in-plane) cross C_SF and become luminous. Subhalos on low-coherence orbits (polar, highly eccentric) remain below C_SF despite their mass. The TBTF subhalos are not too big to fail — they are too low-coherence to shine.

Boylan-Kolchin et al. (2011, 2012) identified the TBTF problem: in CDM simulations, the most massive subhalos have V_max = 30–55 km/s. These halos are too massive to have been reionization-suppressed. They should form stars. Yet the brightest observed Milky Way dwarfs (Fornax, Sculptor, Leo I) have V_max ≈ 15–20 km/s — a factor of 2–3 smaller.

2. Orbital Coherence as Star Formation Gate

2.1 The Coherence of a Subhalo Orbit

Beyond mass, the CTF framework proposes that orbital coherence determines star formation realization in massive subhalos. Define:

C_sub = ⟨cos(Δϕ)⟩ / (1 + e)

where ⟨cos(Δϕ)⟩ is the mean phase alignment of the subhalo orbit with the galactic toroidal field (related to orbital inclination relative to the galactic disk), and e is orbital eccentricity. Circular in-plane orbits give C_sub ≈ 0.7–0.8; polar eccentric orbits give C_sub ≈ 0.1–0.3.

2.2 Why Massive Subhalos Can Be Dark

In standard models, V_max > 30 km/s guarantees star formation because the potential well retains gas against reionization. In the CTF model, a massive subhalo on a polar or highly eccentric orbit can still fall below C_SF despite its mass. Phase decoherence suppresses gas cooling efficiency by repeatedly disrupting the gas coherence through strong tidal forcing at pericenter passages. A massive subhalo that swings through pericenter every few Gyr on a polar orbit is subject to a different coherence environment than a massive subhalo on a stable near-circular in-plane orbit.

The unobserved TBTF subhalos should occupy the polar and eccentric orbital parameter space. Gaia proper motions combined with radial velocities now allow full 3D orbital reconstruction for most known Milky Way satellites — the test is available.

4. The LMC as Coherence-Consistent

The Large Magellanic Cloud — the Milky Way's most massive known satellite — is on a near-polar, mildly eccentric first-passage orbit. It is the most massive and brightest satellite. The CTF resolution: the LMC is massive enough (V_max ≈ 90 km/s) to maintain high self-coherence even on a non-planar orbit. The TBTF subhalos at V_max ≈ 30–40 km/s lack this self-coherence advantage and rely more on orbital coherence — which, on polar eccentric orbits, is insufficient.

5. Milky Way vs. Andromeda Comparison

Andromeda lies in a denser, more coherent local environment than the Milky Way. The prediction: Andromeda should have fewer TBTF-dark massive subhalos and more luminous massive satellites at equivalent mass, because higher environmental coherence raises C_sub for all subhalos regardless of orbit type. The ratio of luminous to dark massive satellites should be higher in Andromeda. PAndAS survey data allow a direct comparison of the luminosity functions above V_max ≈ 20 km/s — a clean test.

6. Detection Through Gravitational Lensing and Stream Perturbations

If TBTF subhalos are dark (present but non-luminous), they should be detectable gravitationally. A dark subhalo at 10⁹–10¹⁰ M☉ produces detectable flux ratio anomalies in multiply-imaged quasars. HST and JWST surveys of lensed systems should detect dark subhalos at CDM-predicted mass function. Additionally, stellar streams (GD-1, Pal 5, the Anticenter Stream) show density variations attributable to dark subhalo encounters consistent with CDM predictions if the TBTF subhalos are dark rather than absent.

7. Falsifiable Predictions

  • Orbital analysis of known Milky Way satellites: luminous satellites should have statistically lower eccentricities and lower inclinations than a random sampling of CDM subhalo orbits — the coherence selection effect.
  • Dark subhalo detection through lensing and stream perturbations should match CDM predictions in the 10⁹–10¹⁰ M☉ range.
  • Andromeda luminosity function at V_max > 20 km/s should exceed Milky Way (corrected for incompleteness and distance).

8. Conclusion

The too-big-to-fail problem is resolved when orbital coherence is recognized as a co-determining factor for star formation alongside mass. Massive subhalos on low-coherence orbits — polar, highly eccentric — fail to accumulate the organizational coherence needed for luminous realization despite their large potential wells. They are not too big to fail. They are too low-coherence to shine. The TBTF subhalos are dark, present, and detectable through the next generation of gravitational lensing, stream perturbation, and proper motion surveys.
