Abstract

The muon anomalous magnetic moment a_μ = (g−2)/2 is one of the most precisely measured and precisely calculated quantities in physics. The experimental average from Brookhaven E821 and Fermilab Muon g-2 Runs 1–3 exceeds the Standard Model prediction by approximately 4.2σ: Δa_μ ≈ 25.1 ± 5.9 × 10⁻¹°. Proposed explanations — supersymmetry, dark photons, leptoquarks, hadronic vacuum polarization errors — each face serious constraints from LHC searches and direct measurements. This paper proposes a geometric resolution: the muon is a toroidal coherence resonator. Its internal toroidal structure contributes an additional precession term to the anomalous magnetic moment absent from the Standard Model's point-particle treatment. The toroidal contribution scales as (r_tor/r_pol)² ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁹ — giving a contribution of order 2.5 × 10⁻⁹, matching the observed anomaly. The electron g-2 remains consistent because (m_e/m_μ)² suppresses the electron correction to ~10⁻¹³. No new particles are required.

The Standard Model prediction includes five-loop QED, electroweak, and hadronic contributions. The hadronic contributions — particularly the leading-order hadronic vacuum polarization (HVP) — carry the largest theoretical uncertainty and are the subject of ongoing controversy between dispersive (data-driven) and lattice QCD approaches.

2. The Toroidal Muon

2.1 Internal Structure and Toroidal Precession

The Standard Model treats the muon as a point particle. In the CTF framework, the muon is a toroidal coherence resonator with characteristic poloidal radius r_pol (related to the muon Compton wavelength λ_μ = 1.87 fm) and toroidal radius r_tor (much smaller). In an external magnetic field, the muon's internal toroidal circulation also precesses, adding a contribution proportional to the toroidal current:

a_μ^{CTF} = a_μ^{SM} + (1/2)(r_tor/r_pol)² · C_μ

2.2 Extracting the Toroidal Aspect Ratio

Δa_μ ≈ 25 × 10⁻¹°  ⇒  (r_tor/r_pol)² ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁹  ⇒  r_tor/r_pol ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁵

The toroidal radius is approximately 10⁻⁴ of the poloidal radius — an extremely thin torus, nearly a circular loop. This small but nonzero ratio is consistent with the muon having subtle internal geometric structure not captured by point-particle QFT.

3. Why the Electron g-2 Is Consistent

The electron g-2 (a_e) agrees with the Standard Model to approximately 10⁻¹² — far more precisely than the muon anomaly. In the CTF framework:

(r_tor/r_pol)²_electron ∝ (m_e/m_μ)² × (r_tor/r_pol)²_muon ≈ (1/207)² × 5 × 10⁻⁹ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹³

The predicted electron toroidal contribution is ~10⁻¹³ — well below current measurement precision (~10⁻¹²). The electron g-2 appears exactly consistent with the Standard Model point-particle calculation because the toroidal correction is too small to observe. This is a key structural coherence of the framework: the anomaly is absent for the electron, present for the muon, and should be larger for the tau — scaling with mass squared.

5. No New Particles Required

The standard explanation paradigm invokes new particles (SUSY smuons, dark photons, leptoquarks) contributing through loop diagrams. The CTF framework requires no new particles. The contribution is geometric — it arises from the muon's internal toroidal precession in the external field, not from virtual loops of undiscovered particles. This aligns with the LHC's repeated failure to find new physics in the mass range that would produce the required Δa_μ.

6. Field Dependence at High B

In the CTF framework, the toroidal coherence C_μ of the muon may change at very high external magnetic fields — much stronger than the 1.45 T storage ring used in g-2 experiments. If C_μ decreases at very high B (because the external field disrupts the muon's internal coherence), the toroidal contribution to a_μ would decrease, and the measured anomaly would show field-dependent suppression. This prediction is not testable with current technology but places the CTF framework within a physically consistent picture.

7. Hadronic Contribution Tensions

A separate source of theoretical uncertainty has emerged from tension between the dispersive (data-driven) determination of the leading-order HVP contribution and recent lattice QCD calculations. The BMW lattice (2021) found a_μ^{HVP,LO} ≈ 707 × 10⁻¹°, shifting the SM prediction upward and reducing the tension to ~1.5σ by some analyses. The CTF geometric interpretation is orthogonal to this controversy — it predicts the additional geometric contribution regardless of how the hadronic tension resolves.

8. Falsifiable Predictions

  • LHC high-luminosity searches for SUSY smuons, dark photons, and leptoquarks should continue to return null results in the mass range relevant to the muon g-2 anomaly.
  • Electron g-2 should remain consistent with the Standard Model to 10⁻¹² precision regardless of future improvements.
  • Tau g-2, if measurable at ~10⁻⁶, should show an anomaly of order (m_τ/m_μ)² × Δa_μ ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁷.
  • Future g-2 experiments at higher magnetic field strengths (if achievable) should show non-linear field dependence in a_μ at the level predicted by the field-coherence coupling.

9. Conclusion

The muon g-2 anomaly is a geometric effect: the muon is a toroidal coherence resonator whose internal structure contributes an additional precession in an external magnetic field. The anomaly scales with (r_tor/r_pol)² ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁹ — a thin-torus geometry consistent with the muon's status as a massive, high-coherence particle. The electron g-2 is consistent because the electron's toroidal correction is suppressed by (m_e/m_μ)² to unobservable levels. The tau anomaly is predicted to be 280× larger than the muon's — a key long-range test. No new particles required. Only the recognition that point-particle QFT may miss sub-perturbative geometric contributions from the internal structure of the leptons themselves.

Δa_μ = (1/2)(r_tor/r_pol)² · C_μ ≈ 25 × 10⁻¹°
