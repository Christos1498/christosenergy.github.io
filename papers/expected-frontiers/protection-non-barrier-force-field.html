Abstract

This white paper presents the CHRISTOS® concept of a non-barrier interaction envelope: a spatially distributed, sensor-driven system intended to detect predefined physical disturbances and, where established coupling mechanisms permit, produce localized responses without requiring a continuous solid wall. The proposed architecture combines calibrated sensing, state estimation, phase-aware actuation, recursive feedback, independent verification, and fault containment. The term “force field” is retained as the research concept’s name, not as a claim of a demonstrated novel force or universal protective capability.

The near-term scientific objective is narrower than protection: determine whether a benign laboratory disturbance can be modified reproducibly in a specified test region, beyond changes predicted by conventional electromagnetic, acoustic, mechanical, thermal, and instrumental models. Prototype 1A establishes metrology and single-platform controls. Prototype 1B investigates interactions among six controlled nodes (R1–R6). Both require preregistration, sham conditions, calibrated independent sensors, uncertainty analysis, and energy accounting. No human exposure, projectile testing, wearable protection claims, or deployment is justified by the current evidence.

1. Purpose, scope, and claim boundary

The central proposal is to investigate protection as a controlled interaction rather than as an assumed impenetrable surface. In its mature hypothetical form, a distributed system would identify the physical signature of a disturbance, determine whether a known coupling mechanism can modify it, and apply only a localized response. The concept does not presume that matter can be stopped without transfer of energy or momentum.

This paper deliberately separates (i) a research metaphor, (ii) an engineering architecture that can be assembled from known components, (iii) measurable conventional coupling effects, and (iv) conjectured new coherence-mediated interactions. Only the first three can presently be described in operational engineering terms; the fourth requires a predictive model and discriminating experiments.

The laboratory program excludes weapons, impacts, high-energy projectiles, human participants, and claims of wearable or dome-scale protection. Initial disturbances are benign acoustic, vibration, electromagnetic, and thermal test signals within documented equipment limits. A functioning six-node controller or a reproducible local response change does not establish protection against impact or projectiles. No experimental apparatus described here is personal protective equipment or appropriate for protective use.

1.1 Primary falsifiable question

Can a predefined disturbance response y(r,t) be changed within a spatially registered region under active control, relative to randomized sham and inactive conditions, after calibration, drift, energy input, and conventional coupling are accounted for?

1.2 Secondary questions

How do phase relationships and node spacing affect measured responses? Is the effect reproducible across instruments and independent laboratories? Does a specified coherence metric add predictive information beyond conventional variables?

2. The non-barrier interaction-envelope concept

2.1 Definition of a non-barrier interaction envelope

The proposed envelope is a bounded region in which sensors, estimation software, and controlled actuators attempt to modify a measurable interaction without relying on an impermeable wall. The term non-barrier describes the intended operational behavior, not an established new state of matter or a demonstrated ability to stop an object. A successful laboratory experiment would require a specified disturbance, a calibrated response variable, a defined spatial domain, and an active-versus-control comparison. The system cannot be credited with selectivity merely because an actuator changes a sensor reading: selectivity requires a predefined target response to change while separately measured non-target responses remain within registered tolerances.

2.2 Geometry as a test variable

Toroidal, circular, and distributed-node arrangements appear in the originating architecture as candidate spatial organizations. Their value is experimental: geometry may affect coupling, interference, field distribution, and measurement coverage. A toroidal drawing alone does not establish a toroidal physical field, a return-flow mechanism, or any particular energy pathway. To evaluate geometry, each arrangement should be compared under matched total input energy, actuator count, sensor positions, environmental conditions, and data-processing rules. Position scans should be made on a reproducible coordinate grid. An apparently improved response is attributable to geometry only after ordinary source directivity, resonances, reflections, and placement effects have been considered.

A wall excludes by mechanical contact. An interaction envelope, if feasible for a particular disturbance, would instead rely on sensing and coupling. The proposed chain is: disturbance → early detection → state estimation → candidate response → bounded actuation → independent outcome measurement. This is a control architecture, not evidence of an invisible barrier.

Selectivity means a controller applies different actions to different predefined, physically measurable disturbance classes. It does not mean the apparatus understands hostile intent or can automatically separate harmless from harmful objects. A selectivity function Λ(z) must be specified using observable variables and validated for each laboratory class.

Spatial localization requires measuring response across a grid, not merely at one favored sensor position. Transparency to unrelated signals is an empirical constraint: unchanged passage must be measured, with confidence bounds, rather than assumed.

3. Governing architecture: sensing, estimation, actuation, verification

3.1 Conventional foundation

Active acoustic cancellation, vibration control, electromagnetic field shaping, and feedback regulation are established categories of engineering. Each has limits on bandwidth, geometry, energy, latency, and medium. The research must specify which physical medium is being controlled before extrapolating to other disturbance classes.

3.2 Proposed extensions

Toroidal geometry, recursive phase organization, and φ-scaled node spacing are candidate experimental variables. They are not prerequisites of the basic instrument, nor evidence of a novel field. The experimental plan should compare them against equal-resource non-toroidal and non-φ controls.

3.3 Two-loop control and independent verification

The inner loop maintains hardware variables within their permitted ranges: output amplitude, timing, temperature, electrical current, and actuator state. The outer loop estimates a disturbance and requests a bounded response. A separate measurement path evaluates the effect; this path should not reuse the same unqualified sensor stream as the controller's error signal. Otherwise the system may simply optimize its own measurement artifact. Every trial should retain raw sensor data, actuator command logs, time stamps, calibration identifiers, and a machine-readable description of the active, inactive, and sham states. An independent safety circuit must be capable of interrupting actuation regardless of software state.

3.4 Failure behavior and uncertainty-aware control

The default behavior under loss of synchronization, sensor saturation, excessive temperature, missing data, or unexplained residuals is a safe de-energized state. Uncertainty is not merely a value to report after testing; it is an operating input that can restrict permitted actuation. Controller stability must be established on a benign load before closed-loop disturbance tests. Saturation, anti-windup, delayed measurements, actuator cross-talk, and failed nodes must be tested individually. No subject, person, animal, or live protective-use scenario belongs in this initial verification sequence.

The proposed architecture has three physical levels: an instrumented test environment; a state model that organizes measured variables; and a bounded actuator interface that can affect a known physical channel. Each level must be independently qualified before any closed-loop experiment.

The primary controller can estimate disturbance phase, amplitude, frequency, position, and uncertainty. A separate verification pathway must measure the physical outcome without relying solely on the controller’s own sensors. An independent stop circuit limits energy, temperature, current, and other exposure variables even if the control software fails.

Two loops are distinguished. The inner loop stabilizes drivers and actuator output; the outer loop estimates environmental response and adjusts candidate control commands. Loop delay, saturation, noise, crosstalk, and instability are design-limiting variables.

3.5 Causal architecture and observable boundaries

The controlled experimental chain is command → driver output → local field or mechanical state → propagation pathway → target response → independent measurement. Every arrow represents a measurable transfer relationship, not an assumed causal effect. A trial cannot isolate the target interaction unless it also records driver state, supply conditions, and environmental coupling. The independent witness instrument should not share the same control loop that defines the primary outcome.

A useful baseline is a multivariable transfer model y(t)=G*u(t)+H*z(t)+ε(t), where u represents recorded actuator inputs, z measured nuisance variables, G and H conventional response operators, and ε unexplained residuals. These operators may be linear only over a declared operating range. When harmonic distortion or saturation is detected, the analysis must use an appropriate nonlinear or state-dependent model rather than labeling the deviation anomalous.

The proposed interaction envelope is operationally the bounded volume and time interval within which a prespecified target response is measured. Its boundaries are defined by the measurement grid, instrument sensitivity, and confidence limits. It must not be drawn as a physical shell or described as a protective zone without independent evidence of the relevant performance.

4. Hypothesis stack and alternative explanations

The research program establishes a graded hypothesis stack from measurement artifacts through ordinary physical coupling to more speculative organization effects. The order matters: no result should be assigned to an exotic mechanism while a lower-level explanation remains sufficient.

Level 0 covers instrumentation and software artifacts. Levels 1–4 cover electromagnetic, mechanical/acoustic, thermal/fluid, and plasma-mediated mechanisms. Higher levels examine geometry dependence, phase organization, recursive dynamics, φ-scaled arrangements, emergent coherence coupling, and the proposed MoR/coherence interaction.

An observation may support an engineering effect without supporting the claimed explanatory model. An active-versus-sham difference, for example, can result from ordinary actuator radiation, mechanical vibration, sensor pickup, temperature drift, unblinded operation, or changed power delivery. Every candidate mechanism therefore needs its own forward prediction and disconfirming observation.

4.1 Competing-model register and discriminating observations

Model M0 is an inactive baseline incorporating ambient drift and instrument noise. Model M1 includes conventional electromagnetic, acoustic, mechanical, and thermal pathways, with parameters estimated from independent calibration or training data. Model M2 is a predeclared extension with an additional measurable state variable and a specified coupling term. M2 must predict an observation not already accommodated by a reasonably calibrated M1.

Candidate discriminators include spatial falloff, frequency dependence, phase dependence, temporal recovery, and response to changes in boundary conditions. A discriminator is scientifically useful only when M1 and M2 predict materially different values before the confirmatory trial. An unexplained residual discovered after data collection is a research lead, not proof of M2.

Model comparison should use held-out data, predictive residuals, parameter uncertainty, and complexity penalties. Report negative results and failed predictions alongside positive ones. If the models are observationally indistinguishable under available instrumentation, the correct outcome is inconclusive.

4.2 Origin of the proposed return-pathway hypothesis

The CHRISTOS® research program originated in a question about whether hazardous or disordered energy could be redirected into an organized return pathway instead of being resisted by a rigid barrier. The initiating thought experiment concerned volcanic energy management: a shell formed around lava might contain material without addressing the continuing pressure and energy driving an eruption. The alternative imagined an inwardly directed, singularity-like organizing region through which the disturbance could be recoupled into a controlled return process. This is a conceptual origin statement, not an observation of such a physical structure.

In this paper, 'singularity-like' describes a proposed concentration or organizing center in a system representation. It does not assert a gravitational singularity, black hole, event horizon, or new force. 'Return' denotes a hypothesized pathway by which an incident disturbance might couple to other measurable degrees of freedom. The destination and conversion of energy must be established experimentally. No disappearance, neutralization, or harmless grounding of energy is assumed.

4.3 From the originating idea to a laboratory hypothesis

A physical implementation must identify an incident quantity d(t), a target response y(t), an actuator command u(t), a spatial region Ω, and a measurable return or redistribution channel r(t). For an acoustic bench, these may be sound pressure, calibrated loudspeaker drive, microphone pressure, and vibrational or acoustic power measured at other locations. For a mechanical bench, they may instead be applied force, actuator force, acceleration, and reaction forces. These examples are conventional research platforms, not protective field demonstrations.

The minimal testable proposition is that a registered control configuration changes a spatially resolved response under matched source input. A stronger proposition, that the change reflects a previously unmodeled return-flow interaction, requires an explicit predictive model and a residual that persists after conventional transfer, absorption, interference, and feedback models are fitted and tested on held-out data.

An observation at one sensor is not sufficient: the same reduction may reflect a shifted interference node or a displaced response maximum. The experiment must survey the operating volume and measure relevant output channels.

4.4 Candidate pathways and conservation constraints

Four conventional pathways can create an apparent non-barrier reduction: destructive interference at a chosen measurement location, mechanical or electromagnetic absorption, redistribution to a different direction or mode, and modification of the disturbance source through feedback or loading. Each pathway predicts a different spatial and energetic signature. The research program should quantify these before interpreting a residual as an additional mechanism.

Define a control volume with incoming power Pin, outgoing power Pout, dissipated power Pheat, and rate of stored-energy change dEstore/dt. Within a properly specified physical boundary and with sign conventions documented, conservation requires an accounting relationship of the form Pin + Pact = Pout + Pheat + dEstore/dt + Punmeasured. Here Punmeasured is an uncertainty or omitted-channel term, not a proposed sink for unexplained energy. The term must shrink as the measurement boundary improves.

The phrase 'decoupling to ground' is permitted only as a description of an identified coupling pathway, such as measured current through a protective-earth conductor or measured vibration transferred to a mechanical foundation. Protective earth is not a generic disposal mechanism for electromagnetic, kinetic, thermal, or acoustic energy.

4.5 Candidate spatial organizations

A toroidal recirculation pattern is one candidate visualization of a return pathway, but it is neither a prerequisite nor a privileged conclusion. Competing configurations include radial, axial, distributed-node, waveguide-mediated, and geometry-independent feedback architectures. Each geometry must be specified by coordinates, dimensions, source and receiver locations, boundary conditions, and operating frequency range.

Geometric comparisons must be normalized for total supplied energy, actuator count, sensor coverage, occupied volume, material composition, and mechanical supports. If the candidate toroidal arrangement performs better, the next question is whether a conventional model predicts the difference. A performance advantage alone does not identify a new field mechanism.

Any visual depiction of an 'envelope' represents a hypothesized region of control or measurement. It must not be mistaken for an impenetrable physical surface, a certified exclusion zone, or a demonstrated human protection boundary.

4.6 A forward-model hierarchy

Model M0 represents the disturbance-only response, including measured background and source drift. Model M1 adds the measured conventional response of the actuators and the sensor–actuator transfer matrix. Model M2 includes conventional nonlinearities, temperature dependence, structural coupling, and closed-loop control effects. Model M3 is a prospectively specified additional return-flow term with defined parameters and observables. The hierarchy is a comparison framework; M3 is not justified merely by being more flexible.

For sensor j at location xj, a provisional frequency-domain representation is Yj(ω) = Hjd(ω)D(ω) + Σi Hji(ω)Ui(ω) + Nj(ω). A candidate additional term Qj(ω; θ, xj) may be tested only after its functional form and parameter restrictions are fixed in advance. Model selection must penalize complexity and evaluate held-out predictive error, calibration uncertainty, and sensitivity to measurement location.

A physically persuasive model must predict a measurable pattern that the conventional alternatives cannot reproduce within stated uncertainty. A residual confined to one frequency bin, one sensor, one geometry, or one analysis choice should be treated as a possible artifact until independently replicated.

4.7 Prospective discriminating experiments

Experiment A compares disturbance-only, sham, fixed-actuation, and closed-loop conditions with an independently monitored source. It asks whether any response change is repeatable and whether source strength remains constant. Experiment B maps the response across a three-dimensional grid and tests whether an apparent local reduction is offset by increased response elsewhere. Experiment C characterizes the complete sensor–actuator transfer matrix with the disturbance absent and uses it to predict the combined response.

Experiment D compares toroidal, radial, and control geometries at matched total electrical input and registered positions. Experiment E changes the boundary conditions, such as mounting stiffness or absorbing material, to test whether the measured effect follows ordinary structural or wave propagation. Experiment F transfers a frozen model to a second laboratory or a separately assembled platform without retuning on the validation data.

Every experiment requires a primary outcome, fixed measurement bandwidth, calibrated uncertainty, exclusion rules, sample-size rationale, and predefined pass/fail/inconclusive interpretation. An unexplained difference is not automatically evidence for return-flow organization.

4.8 Falsification, safety, and scope of inference

The laboratory hypothesis fails its stated test if the registered effect is absent within the designed sensitivity, reverses under controls in a way predicted by ordinary physics, or cannot be reproduced after accounting for instrument and source variability. An inconclusive result arises when the uncertainty is too large to decide. Neither outcome should be relabeled as confirmation of a hidden mechanism.

Prototype 1A and Prototype 1B are restricted to benign, bounded research disturbances and non-human test articles. No person should rely on a prototype to stop impacts, projectiles, radiation, harmful acoustic exposure, or other hazardous events. Human-worn and room-scale applications are future design concepts that require independent physical validation and safety certification before any practical protection claim.

Accordingly, the paper advances a research question: can a controlled distributed system produce a reproducible, spatially characterized disturbance response beyond a well-specified conventional model? It does not report a verified force field or a proven new interaction.

5. Operational definitions and mathematical model

5.1 Controlled effect

Define Δy(r,t) = yactive(r,t) − ycontrol(r,t) for the same calibrated response variable at matched positions and prespecified observation windows. Active and control measurements are acquired in randomized, balanced sessions. The active-minus-control sign is retained consistently in every reported estimate, with uncertainty intervals accounting for within-session correlation and shared calibration error.

5.2 Response-reduction measure

For comparable observation windows and the same calibrated response variable, define RdB = 10 log₁₀(Mcontrol/Mactive), where M = mean[y(t)²] is the mean-square response with units equal to the square of the measured response. This is a response-index ratio, not a measured energy transfer. Report RdB only when both indices are positive and above a prespecified validated measurement floor; otherwise report a bounded/censored result with the instrument floor and uncertainty rather than an infinite reduction.

5.3 Energy and delay

For a declared electrical-system boundary, net electrical energy entering the boundary is Eelec = ∫Σᵢ sᵢvᵢ(t)iᵢ(t)dt, where sᵢ defines the positive-inward port sign, v and i are simultaneously sampled, and the integration interval is stated. Include initial/final stored energy and all separately measured outward transfers when closing the balance. Total loop latency includes sampling, computation, communication, driver and actuator response; for a pure delay τ and e^(+jωt) convention, the phase shift is −2πfτ radians. A dispersive actuator cannot be represented by one delay at all frequencies.

5.4 Uncertainty

For uncorrelated inputs, combined standard uncertainty can be approximated by uc²(y) ≈ Σ(∂f/∂xj)²u²(xj); correlated inputs require covariance terms. Calibration, timing, environmental drift, spatial positioning, and model selection contribute to uncertainty.

5.5 A dimensionally explicit phase model

For actuator i, an illustrative drive command is ui(t) = Ai(t) cos[2π fi t + φi(t)], where ui is the commanded output in its declared actuator-specific units, Ai is its amplitude, fi is frequency in hertz, t is time in seconds, and φi is phase in radians. A drive waveform is not itself a model of a new field. Its physical output must be measured by a calibrated transducer with a documented transfer function. Relative phase is meaningful only if signals share a traceable time base and phase estimates have stated uncertainty. For example, at 200 Hz, one degree of phase corresponds to 1/(360×200) seconds, or approximately 13.9 microseconds; this is an illustrative conversion, not a prescribed tolerance.

5.6 Measurand, effect size, and inference

Let y(t) denote a calibrated response variable, such as displacement in meters or acceleration in meters per second squared. Define the primary effect as the difference between preregistered summary statistics of active and matched control trials. A reduction metric expressed in decibels, RdB = 10 log10(Pcontrol/Pactive), is meaningful only when P is consistently defined as mean-square response in the same units squared, over equivalent windows and bandwidth. If a response is close to the sensor noise floor, the ratio can become unstable and must not be interpreted as physical suppression. Report absolute effect, uncertainty interval, noise floor, and raw response distributions alongside any normalized metric.

5.7 Model comparison and residual diagnostics

Before collecting confirmatory data, specify a conventional forward model that includes known electromagnetic, acoustic, mechanical, thermal, and instrumental pathways relevant to the selected actuator. The proposed architecture must be assessed against this model and at least one simpler alternative, such as fixed-phase open-loop control. Residuals should be examined for timing drift, frequency-dependent artifacts, spatial correlation, and unexplained environmental changes. A statistically significant residual is not sufficient evidence of a novel interaction; the residual must be reproducible, independently measurable, and inconsistent with plausible conventional explanations under predeclared tests.

Mathematical symbols are useful only when they denote measurable quantities. The first experiment must name its response variable y(r,t), its SI units, calibrated instrument, spatial coordinates, sampling rate, time reference, and analysis window. A generic “field strength” without a measurand is not an acceptable endpoint.

For N measured oscillatory channels with phases φᵢ(t), the framework proposes a dimensionless phase-order parameter Cφ(t) = |(1/N) Σ exp(jφᵢ(t))|. Values between zero and one indicate phase dispersion or alignment of those channels. This parameter is a signal statistic; it does not establish a universal coherence field.

A command waveform for node i may be written uᵢ(t) = Aᵢ(t) cos[2πfᵢt + φᵢ(t)]. The physical meaning and units of uᵢ and Aᵢ must be declared, and actual actuator output phase must be measured independently of the digital command.

5.8 Phase uncertainty, synchronization, and dimensional analysis

For a sinusoidal component at frequency f in hertz, a positive pure delay Δt in seconds contributes a signed phasor shift Δφ = −2πfΔt under the e^(+iωt) convention; the phase-error magnitude is |Δφ| = 2πf|Δt|. At 200 Hz, a one-degree magnitude corresponds to approximately 13.9 microseconds. The sign must be reversed if the opposite phasor convention is used. This example is not an apparatus tolerance.

Phase should be estimated from simultaneously sampled reference and response channels, with a stated algorithm, analysis window, signal-to-noise threshold, and treatment of phase wrapping. Report clock jitter, channel skew, timestamp uncertainty, sampling drift, and phase-estimator bias separately where possible. A nominal software phase command is not evidence of physical phase at a node.

Every equation in the experimental record must carry units. Amplitude Ai has units determined by the commanded physical output; frequency fi is in Hz; phase φi is dimensionless in radians; voltage V is in volts; current I is in amperes; time is in seconds; and integrated electrical input energy is in joules. Any proposed coherence quantity C requires its own explicit operational definition and units before it enters a predictive equation.

5.9 From hypothesis to a predictive forward model

A proposed interaction envelope is scientifically testable only if a forward model predicts the response of a specified target under a specified disturbance. Let d(t) be the independently measured disturbance input, u(t) the actuator command, and y(t) the calibrated target response. The conventional reference model is y0(t) = Gd[d](t) + Gu[u](t) + ε(t), where Gd and Gu represent measured disturbance and actuator transfer operators and ε contains bounded noise and unmodeled effects. For a linear time-invariant approximation, frequency-domain measurements permit Y0(ω) = Hd(ω)D(ω) + Hu(ω)U(ω) + Ε(ω). This is a local engineering approximation, not an assertion that the full apparatus is linear.

The proposed extension may be represented provisionally by an additional term qθ[d,u,x,t], giving y1(t) = y0(t) + qθ[d,u,x,t]. The parameter set θ and the function qθ are placeholders until an independently specified constitutive relation and boundary conditions are supplied. Merely fitting qθ to an observed residual after the experiment is insufficient: a new mechanism must generate preregistered, out-of-sample predictions that outperform conventional alternatives with comparable model complexity.

5.10 Defining an observable organization index

The word coherence is not assigned an independent energy or force unit in this paper. A measurable candidate is the magnitude-squared coherence γ²xy(f) = |Sxy(f)|²/[Sxx(f)Syy(f)], estimated from two calibrated time series with declared windows, averaging, leakage controls, and confidence intervals. It is dimensionless and ranges from zero to one for valid spectral estimates. It describes statistical linear association at frequency f, not proof of a new field or a protective effect.

A second candidate is phase-locking value PLV(f) = |N⁻¹ Σk exp(i[φx,k(f) − φy,k(f)])|, where phases are extracted using a declared method over N independent or appropriately modeled segments. PLV is also dimensionless. Shared clocks, reference leakage, common forcing, and narrowband filtering can create high apparent synchronization. The study must compare these indices with independently measured target-response changes and must not redefine an index after examining results.

Interpretive limit for phase-order metrics: a value of Cφ near unity indicates concentration of the sampled phases under the stated weighting and sampling protocol. It is not, by itself, a measure of force, transferred energy, energy density, or a return-flow channel. Any proposed physical coupling requires a separately defined quantity with SI units, a predictive law, calibrated measurements, and conventional-model controls.

5.11 Null models and competing mechanisms

Model M0 is the best supported conventional model for the registered apparatus, including sensor dynamics, source loading, actuator cross-talk, structural vibration, electromagnetic pickup, thermal drift, and known nonlinearities. Model M1 is a conventional but more flexible model allowing interaction terms and frequency-dependent coupling. Model M2 is the specifically declared non-barrier extension. These models must be fitted using training data and compared on held-out trials, preferably with independent replication.

A useful preregistered contrast is ΔL = L(M0) − L(M2), where L is a declared predictive loss evaluated on held-out observations. A positive value favors M2 only with respect to that loss and those data; it does not alone demonstrate new physics. Complexity penalties, calibration uncertainty, and data leakage must be included. Report the performance of M1 as well: a conventional interaction model may explain residual structure without introducing a new mechanism.

5.12 Geometric and spatial prediction requirements

Let r denote a surveyed position in the measurement volume and let Y(r,f) be the complex target response at frequency f. Spatial claims require a preregistered map A(r,f) = |Yactive(r,f)|/|Ysham(r,f)|, with the same source drive, acquisition gain, environmental state, and analysis window. Ratios become unstable near a zero sham denominator, so minimum signal-to-noise rules and absolute response maps are mandatory.

Toroidal, spherical, planar, and irregular node arrangements are candidate geometries, not assumed physical necessities. A geometric hypothesis must specify where maxima, minima, nodal surfaces, and phase reversals are expected before data collection. A response pattern that follows cable routing, support structures, or sensor orientation should be tested as a conventional artifact. Mapping only selected favorable points would not support a spatial organization claim.

5.13 Timing and the proposed 17-second interval

The frequently discussed 17-second lock is treated solely as a preregisterable candidate time scale. Define a lock indicator L(t) from a fixed combination of measurable phase dispersion, response stability, and actuator state; specify thresholds before observing confirmatory data. Then define τlock as the first time L(t) remains true for a declared persistence interval. A testable 17-second hypothesis predicts the distribution of τlock across repeated trials, rather than retrospectively selecting a convenient onset time.

For a pure delay, the magnitude of the phase error is |Δφ| = 2πf|Δt|. At 200 Hz, one degree corresponds to approximately 13.9 microseconds. The signed shift under the e^(+iωt) phasor convention is negative for a positive delay. This arithmetic illustrates the importance of shared-clock validation; it is not an independently established 17-second effect.

5.14 Causal identification and equal-input comparisons

The primary causal contrast is the target response under a randomized active configuration versus a physically matched sham configuration while the independently monitored disturbance remains constant. A second contrast holds total measured actuator input energy fixed while changing the phase pattern. The latter tests organization of the same input rather than confusing a larger response with greater delivered power. Both contrasts require checks for altered source impedance and actuator-to-sensor electrical leakage.

For repeated trials indexed by j, define δj = T(yactive,j) − T(ysham,j), where T is a fixed scalar response functional (for example, band-limited RMS acceleration). The estimator δ̄ and its interval must reflect trial pairing, temporal autocorrelation, instrument uncertainty, and any clustering by node or session. Statistical significance alone does not establish engineering significance; the minimum practically important effect and acceptable uncertainty must be registered.

5.15 Energy, momentum, and dimensional closure

For each electrical input channel, instantaneous real power is pi(t) = vi(t)ii(t), and integrated input energy is Ein = Σi∫pi(t)dt over the same trial interval. Report the sampling bandwidth, voltage-current timing alignment, polarity conventions, and uncertainty. Where the experiment is mechanical, the energy and momentum ledgers must also consider source work, target motion, fixtures, damping, acoustics, electromagnetic coupling, stored energy, and heat.

A decrease in one target observable is not energy disappearance. A localized response can fall because of destructive interference, changed boundary conditions, absorption, or redistribution to unmonitored channels. The experiment therefore reports both the target effect and the closure residual B = Ein + Esource − Emeasured_outputs − ΔEstored − Q, together with a defensible uncertainty interval. B is an accounting diagnostic, not a proposed new source of energy.

5.16 Derivation gap and conditions for a physical theory

At present the proposed architecture provides a research hypothesis and control-system concepts, not a validated field equation that predicts selective disturbance decoupling from first principles. To close that gap, a proposed field variable must have a precise operational definition, dimensions, evolution equation, source terms, constitutive law, boundary conditions, and a measurable coupling to ordinary matter. It must also recover accepted behavior in regimes where conventional theories are well tested.

A future candidate equation must produce numerical predictions without adjusting free parameters separately for each experiment. The required sequence is: specify the equation and parameters; solve for a registered apparatus geometry; predict response and uncertainty; compare against calibrated observations and conventional alternatives; repeat at an independent laboratory. Until this sequence is met, the non-barrier interaction envelope remains an exploratory engineering hypothesis.

5.17 State-space identification and measurable return channels

Let x(t) denote measured plant states, d(t) the independently monitored benign disturbance, u(t) the actuator commands, and y(t) the observed sensor vector. A conventional linear baseline is dx/dt = Ax + Bd d + Bu u + w, with y = Cx + Dd d + Du u + v. The matrices must be calibrated with consistent units; w and v denote model and measurement errors. This is a standard identification model, not a new physical law. A separate vector r(t) must record candidate redistribution channels, including mechanical support forces, additional acoustic receivers, electrical return currents, or heat, as appropriate. A reduced y reading alone does not establish where energy went.

5.18 Six-node complex coupling matrix

At angular frequency ω define Hji(ω) as the complex transfer from actuator i to sensor j. The conventional prediction is Y(ω) = H(ω)U(ω) + Hd(ω)D(ω) + N(ω). Characterize all six columns independently, then verify predictions for all fifteen two-node combinations and finally the six-node assembly. Compare complex phasors, not summed magnitudes. Pairwise residuals can arise from saturation, loading, structural coupling, drift, nonlinearities, or sensor cross-talk. A residual is not by itself evidence of a proposed additional interaction.

5.19 Equal-input phase trials and selection bias

Equal voltage or nominal drive amplitude does not imply equal real electrical input or equal radiated output when impedances change with coupling. Define and monitor the normalization quantity before comparing phase patterns. A registered response objective may be J(φ) = Σj wj ∫_B |Yj(ω;φ)|² dω for fixed weights, sensor grid, and frequency band B. This is a response index, not a universal protection measure. Optimize on a discovery dataset and freeze the selected phase vector before confirmation on held-out locations and runs. Otherwise selection bias will inflate apparent improvements.

5.20 Feedback stability and independent safety

For an identified plant P(s) and controller K(s), examine loop transfer L(s) = P(s)K(s), including phase and gain margins, transport delays, saturation, and unmodeled high-frequency behavior. Short successful trials do not prove stability. A sensor used for feedback optimization should not be the sole sensor used to establish efficacy. Independent measurement channels are required. Hardware interlocks must shut down actuation when measured electrical, thermal, or mechanical limits are exceeded; no safety claim may rely on uninterrupted controller operation.

5.21 Coherence metrics and physical interpretation

Magnitude-squared coherence between signals a and b is γ²_ab(f) = |Sab(f)|²/[Saa(f)Sbb(f)] where the denominator is nonzero. This dimensionless spectral statistic depends on windowing, averaging, and stationarity. Common drive signals, leakage, and ordinary interference can produce high coherence. Coherence therefore cannot serve as evidence for a new field without a separately specified physical quantity, units, governing dynamics, coupling law, and discriminating prediction.

5.22 Spatial integration and uncertainty

Define a registered spatial response index E_Ω = Σj wj ∫_B |Y(xj,ω)|² dω with fixed quadrature weights and frequency band. Unless calibrated through an appropriate physical constitutive relation, E_Ω is not measured energy in joules. Compare active and control conditions with uncertainty intervals that include calibration, covariance, spatial sampling, repeatability, and bandwidth limits. A reduction at one receiver may correspond to an increase elsewhere; the entire mapped region must be reported.

5.23 Explicit theoretical derivation gap

A provisional extension y = yconventional + q_θ(d,u,x,t) is useful only if q_θ has a prespecified functional form, physical dimensions matching y, constrained parameters, and predictions made before measurement. Defining q_θ as whatever residual appears is circular and cannot identify a mechanism. The present paper does not derive q_θ from a validated return-flow field equation or identify an independently observed return-flow carrier. This remains an open theoretical problem.

5.24 Mathematical decision rules

Freeze the primary endpoint, spatial grid, bandwidth, model alternatives, sample-size rationale, uncertainty method, and minimum relevant effect before confirmatory trials. A novel interaction claim would require held-out predictions that outperform comparably constrained conventional models, survive calibration and interference controls, and replicate on independently assembled hardware. Absence of the predicted signature under adequate sensitivity counts against the registered hypothesis. An underpowered result remains inconclusive.

5.25 Equation assumptions and sign-consistency register

Phase: with exp[i(ωt − k·r)], a positive temporal delay contributes −ωΔt and a positive displacement along propagation contributes −kΔr. Phase-error magnitudes remain positive. Changing the temporal or spatial phasor convention requires changing the corresponding signed formula consistently.

Power: P(t) = Σi Vi(t)Ii(t) is signed instantaneous electrical power at the chosen boundary. Net work, gross drawn energy, returned energy, thermal conversion and stored energy are separately defined; none may be counted twice.

Attenuation: the 10log₁₀ formula applies to comparable positive mean-square or power indices. Receiver-specific cancellation is not a global energy budget; the result must be paired with a registered spatial measurement region and source-level control.

Uncertainty: the independent unit for a confirmatory test is the preregistered experimental replicate (for example, a session or independently reset configuration), not each correlated sensor sample. Instrument floor and calibration uncertainty must be propagated into reported bounds.

Publication control: the preceding equations are conventional engineering approximations with stated domains. No Christfield-specific physical constitutive law, unique quantitative protective prediction, or independent experimental result is supplied by these calculations.

6.5 Sensor and actuator transfer-function identification

For every input and output channel, identify the transfer function over the intended measurement band. The minimum record includes gain, phase delay, bandwidth, linear range, noise floor, saturation behavior, and environmental sensitivity. Use calibrated reference stimuli whose amplitudes and timing can be independently observed. An actuator command is not a measurement of delivered physical output; a commanded phase is not necessarily the phase at the test volume. Model the complete chain from digital command through driver, transducer, propagation medium, target, and sensor. Repeat characterization with the actuator disabled to quantify electrical pickup and sensor self-response. Store calibration curves with device serial number, date, method, and uncertainty.

6.6 Data acquisition and timing topology

Specify a common timebase or a documented synchronization method for all channels. A phase error is jointly determined by oscillator phase noise, sample-clock offset, trigger latency, transport delay, and timestamp uncertainty. At frequency f, a timing uncertainty δt contributes a phase uncertainty δφ = 2πfδt radians; the laboratory must report both values rather than claiming synchronization from nominal sample rates. Record raw samples, channel labels, sampling rates, anti-alias filter settings, firmware versions, and clock diagnostics. Every analysis file must trace to immutable raw data and a reproducible processing script. Any lost samples, timestamp discontinuities, or undocumented resampling disqualify the affected trial from confirmatory analysis.

6.7 The return-pathway interface as a measurable engineering boundary

The return-pathway hypothesis becomes an engineering proposition only after a physical boundary and an observable transport channel are specified. The proposed apparatus is therefore partitioned into four experimentally accessible regions: disturbance source, interaction region, return or redistribution pathway, and surrounding environment. Each region has a coordinate system, independently calibrated sensors, and a defined uncertainty contribution. The term return pathway must not substitute for a missing measurement channel.

At the boundary of the interaction region, record the incident and outgoing quantities appropriate to the selected modality. In acoustics these may include pressure and particle velocity, from which intensity can be estimated under validated assumptions. In mechanics they may include force and velocity at mounts and supports. In electrical systems they may include synchronized voltage and current. Cross-domain claims require a conversion model and calibrated transducers rather than equating unlike sensor amplitudes.

A candidate pathway is identified by a measurable change in transport, storage, or dissipation. The simplest valid result is a reproducible redistribution of response under controlled actuation. A more ambitious mechanism claim requires a prospective physical model predicting where the redistributed energy or momentum appears and how the prediction changes with geometry, frequency, and boundary conditions.

6.8 Hardware-to-hypothesis mapping and subsystem roles

The proposed system includes a disturbance generator, reference monitors, spatially registered response sensors, a bounded actuator bank, a clocked data-acquisition system, a supervisory controller, and a separate safety interlock. The control software shall preserve a mode in which feedback is disabled while all instrumentation remains active. A sham condition shall reproduce the ordinary cues of an active trial while withholding the designated physical actuation. 6.9 Spatial geometry, transfer paths, and baseline identification

Use a laboratory coordinate frame with documented origin, axes, reference surfaces, and surveyed node positions. 6.10 Control hierarchy and safety-independent shutdown

The controller is divided into an estimation layer, a bounded actuation layer, and an independent supervisory safety layer. The estimation layer calculates registered observables from calibrated sensor streams. The actuation layer may run open-loop commands or a stable feedback law whose gains and saturation behavior are frozen before confirmatory tests. The safety layer monitors current, voltage, temperature, unexpected motion, timing faults, and communication loss without relying on the hypothesis-specific estimator.

On loss of synchronization, sensor saturation, uncontrolled oscillation, thermal limit excursion, or interlock activation, the system shall transition to a defined non-actuating state and preserve an event log. Fault injection during commissioning shall demonstrate this response. These requirements establish laboratory equipment safety; they do not imply that the system provides protection against an external hazard.

Control-loop performance must be evaluated with ordinary stability and robustness methods. A reduction at the target sensor caused by conventional feedback is a valid engineering result, but it cannot by itself be attributed to a new return-flow mechanism. Use a fixed coordinate reference and a documented three-dimensional measurement grid. For each trial, archive the commanded phase vector, measured output phase vector, environmental state, and independently acquired response map. A geometric pattern is meaningful only if its magnitude exceeds instrument and positioning uncertainty and if it survives a rotated-layout or relocated-sensor control. Compare observed spatial structure with a conventional wave or field model at identical drive settings. The experiment should quantify whether the six-node arrangement improves a specified response beyond the best matched single-node or simpler multi-node baseline, not merely beyond an inactive condition.

Prototype 1B adds six characterized nodes, R1–R6, to investigate whether spatial arrangement, phase relationships, and coupling produce reproducible changes in the measured disturbance field. The six-node research configuration must not be confused with the separate 16-oscillator Field AI program.

Each node is qualified individually before multi-node operation. Pairwise coupling is then characterized, followed by subsets and the full array. Test order is randomized to avoid confounding geometry or phase conditions with temperature, battery state, drift, or operator expectations.

Equal-power controls are essential. An apparent six-node advantage can arise from increased total input energy, additional radiating area, altered sensor placement, or simple interference. Comparisons should include matched total electrical input, matched active-node count where appropriate, deliberately detuned phases, and alternative geometries. Define the matched-power comparison at the measured electrical input terminals, with matched duty cycle and acquisition window. Report electrical power and local response separately; equal drive commands do not guarantee equal real input power.

The central outcome is not a visually striking field map but a reproducible difference between prespecified conditions with uncertainty, conventional-model residuals, and independent replication.

8.3 Six-node factorial characterization and equal-energy comparison

The six-node arrangement R1–R6 is an experimental configuration, not a demonstrated protective field. First characterize each node separately at matched drive limits, then selected pairs, three-node subsets, and the full array. This ordering permits estimation of individual effects, coupling terms, and aggregate behavior while exposing failed or drifting nodes.

For each array state, record a measured input-power vector P=(P1,…,P6), the phase vector Φ=(φ1,…,φ6), the node positions ri, and the observed target response. Compare conditions at equal measured total input energy and, where feasible, equal sensor distance and effective radiating area. Include a scrambled-phase condition, an inactive-node condition, and a conventional superposition prediction.

Interaction effects can be represented as the measured response minus the sum or modeled combination of individually calibrated contributions. Such residuals can arise from ordinary nonlinear coupling, impedance changes, or sensor saturation. Independent validation must first eliminate those explanations. The six-node bench must not be conflated with the separate 16-oscillator Field AI concept. For a common baseline y₀ and separately measured individual-node responses yᵢ under matched conditions, the additive null prediction is ypred = y₀ + Σᵢ(yᵢ − y₀). Compare complex responses where phase is measured. This null can fail from ordinary mutual coupling, resonance or nonlinearity and is not a unique test of Christfield Dynamics.

8.4 R1–R6 calibration and exchangeability test

Treat the six nominal nodes as six distinct devices until measured otherwise. For each node record resonance peaks, quality factor where meaningful, electrical impedance, phase response, output amplitude, temperature drift, and mechanical mounting sensitivity. Repeat a reference test after exchanging two node positions. If a spatial feature follows the device rather than the position, it suggests a device-specific cause; if it follows the position, investigate fixture modes, boundary conditions, and propagation geometry. The experiment should include a deliberately detuned node and an unpowered substitute to expose dependence on tuning and hardware presence. These controls are especially important when spatial symmetry is part of the proposed explanation.

8.5 Pairwise coupling and six-node matrix

Measure the response to each node independently, then to each unordered pair, before all-node operation. For a six-node set there are fifteen unique pairs. Record a complex frequency-dependent transfer matrix Hij(f), where i denotes an observed channel and j a commanded node, with gain and phase reported separately. The linear superposition prediction is constructed from independently measured single-node responses using the same total-energy normalization and boundary conditions. A residual between measured combined response and linear prediction is not, by itself, evidence for the proposed mechanism; nonlinear drivers, shared supplies, structural modes, feedback interaction, and sensor saturation must be tested. Preserve the complete response matrix, including null and unfavorable observations.

8.6 Spatial survey and alternative geometry

A toroidal depiction is a candidate way to visualize spatial organization, not an exclusive premise of the originating return-flow hypothesis. Compare registered geometric hypotheses, including no organized structure, ordinary source superposition, fixture-dependent standing-wave patterns, and the proposed organized return-flow pattern. Use a survey grid that includes the central region, radial and axial transects, and points outside the anticipated envelope. The same grid must be measured under inactive and sham conditions. Analysis should use a coordinate-registered field map, not selected images. Any claimed central return pathway would require a specified transported quantity, a measurable flux or flow observable, conservation accounting, and a predictive model that outperforms conventional alternatives on held-out measurements.

8.7 Six-node release and stop conditions

Prototype 1B may proceed from isolated-node characterization to coupled operation only when channel cross-talk, clock synchronization, power limits, sensor range, and emergency shutdown have passed documented checks. A thermal excursion, unexpected electrical current, uncontrolled oscillation, actuator saturation, loss of synchronization, or invalid independent disturbance monitor triggers a stop and incident record. No trial involving a person, projectile, vehicle, or hazardous disturbance is authorized by this research plan. Completion of six-node characterization establishes a laboratory dataset, not a verified wearable or environmental protection system.

8.8 Six-node prediction matrix and model discrimination

A six-node experiment admits 15 distinct node pairs, 20 distinct triples, and 64 on/off activation masks including all-off and all-on. Exhaustive phase sweeps across every mask may be impractical, so the confirmatory design must be chosen in advance to cover individual transfer functions, pair interactions, selected higher-order combinations, and full-system behavior. Exploratory screening and confirmatory replication should use separate data sets.

Let Hji(f) denote the calibrated complex transfer function from node i to independent sensor j. A linear superposition prediction is Yj,pred(f) = Σi Hji(f)Ui(f) + Dj(f), with Dj representing the measured disturbance-only response. Deviations are summarized as Rj(f) = Yj,observed(f) − Yj,pred(f). Before interpreting Rj, test amplifier saturation, mechanical nonlinearity, heating, source-load feedback, and sensor dynamic range. Compare a conventional nonlinear interaction model before assigning residuals to a new mechanism.

For spatial surveys, specify coordinate fiducials, sensor orientation, grid density, repeated reference positions, and a randomized measurement order. The published result should include all measured grid points and uncertainty maps, not only a visually persuasive contour. Any toroidal-looking pattern must be compared with predictions for the same geometry under ordinary wave propagation and boundary conditions.

8.9 Six-node topology and observable return channels

Prototype 1B uses six separately characterized nodes, R1 through R6, arranged in a recorded coordinate frame. Each node has a command input, a measured electrical input, a local response monitor, and a temperature or other relevant safety monitor. The six-node layout is a test instrument for spatial organization, not evidence that six nodes are necessary or sufficient for a protective envelope.

For every configuration, the laboratory shall specify a candidate return or redistribution observable. Depending on the modality, this may be energy carried toward a secondary receiver region, force transmitted through a support, dissipated heat, or a spatially redistributed wavefield. If no channel can be measured, the study must report only the observed response change and leave the proposed return mechanism unresolved.

Node-to-node coupling shall be measured in both directions where the system permits. Apparent asymmetry may arise from directional transducers, gain mismatch, boundary conditions, or measurement timing. It should not be interpreted as a preferred energy-flow direction without calibrated bidirectional tests.

8.10 Phase organization and matched-input controls

The six-node phase program should include synchronous operation, predefined phase gradients, randomized phase assignments, and independently controlled node phases. The frequency, amplitude, phase, and timing of every channel shall be measured rather than inferred from commanded values. The total delivered input, not merely the sum of software amplitude settings, is the normalization variable for equal-energy comparisons.

At each registered configuration, acquire repeated source-only, inactive-hardware, sham, active open-loop, and active feedback observations where applicable. Randomize confirmatory run order and log thermal drift, source output, and instrument gain. If a phase pattern produces a local reduction, inspect the full spatial map for compensating maxima and compare the outcome with the measured conventional transfer matrix.

An interaction residual may be reported only with its confidence or uncertainty interval, the full list of evaluated configurations, and a held-out confirmation result. Optimizing phases on the same dataset used to claim discovery inflates the apparent effect and must be identified as exploratory.

8.11 Candidate return-flow signatures and competing predictions

The proposed organizing pathway can be represented by a candidate transport signature S(x,ω) defined before data collection. For example, S may describe the direction and magnitude of measured intensity or energy flux in a selected plane. Its definition must follow the physics of the disturbance modality; an acoustic intensity vector, a mechanical power flow, and an electromagnetic Poynting vector are not interchangeable quantities.

The conventional null predicts the spatial pattern from measured source, actuator, boundary, and transfer characteristics. A candidate return-flow model must make a narrower, prospectively testable prediction such as a specified spatial gradient, a reproducible phase-dependent transfer feature, or a geometry-dependent redistribution that survives ordinary model uncertainty. The paper does not yet provide a derived function that meets this standard.

An inward-pointing vector pattern alone would not prove a singularity-like center. Focusing, standing waves, absorption, and boundary reflections can all produce convergent local patterns. The decisive evidence would be a quantitative model comparison across altered boundary conditions, held-out geometries, and independent instrumentation.

8.12 Prototype 1B decision gate and replication package

The exit decision for Prototype 1B has three outcomes: proceed to independent replication, redesign and retest, or discontinue the tested hypothesis. Proceeding requires that instruments and interlocks pass qualification, a preregistered primary response survives controls with a sufficiently narrow uncertainty interval, and the observed pattern is reproducible on held-out runs. A claim of a new physical interaction requires additional model discrimination beyond this gate.

The replication package shall contain mechanical coordinates, hardware and firmware identifiers, calibration certificates, raw timestamped data, source-monitor records, environmental logs, analysis code and version hashes, preregistration, exclusions, model fits, and uncertainty budgets. A second laboratory must be able to reproduce the registered analysis without access to unpublished tuning decisions.

Failure to detect a novel residual does not make the engineering work valueless. The program may still yield a useful conventional active-control platform or a reliable measurement system. Those outcomes should be published accurately, without converting a negative mechanism test into an unsupported protective claim.

8.13 Six-node held-out identification protocol

Characterize R1–R6 individually, estimate the complete six-input transfer matrix, test all fifteen pairs, and then run the full array under matched measured input. Record serial numbers, locations, mounting conditions, cable paths, clock synchronization, temperatures, and calibration files. Freeze model parameters and phase patterns before evaluation on an independent spatial sensor grid. Reverse node assignments and change supports in diagnostic trials to investigate ordinary coupling.

8.14 Return-channel evidence and advancement criteria

Instrument a plausible redistribution pathway for the chosen benign modality: support reactions for mechanical testing, boundary sound-power measurements for acoustic testing, or real electrical power and dissipated heat for electrical testing. Advance to independent replication only after the conventional-model baseline, uncertainty budget, safety checks, and registered endpoint are complete. If a local response decreases while output increases elsewhere, report redistribution rather than energy disappearance. Do not infer hazardous-event protection from benign bench measurements.

8.15 Prototype 1B assembly and acceptance dossier

Build six node records with independent serial numbers, component revisions, physical dimensions, mounting positions, electrical connections, calibration certificates, and firmware identifiers. Verify each node on the qualified 1A measurement chain before integration. Record every connection and cable route on the as-built diagram.

Commission R1–R6 one at a time, then in pairs, then as a complete array. Confirm that summed electrical input and measured output are consistent with the selected normalization rule. Measure transfer functions at the intended operating positions and repeat after cable or mounting changes that could alter coupling.

Full-array operation is prohibited until individual node fault tests, leakage tests, and safety shutdown checks pass. The assembled six-node platform remains a benign research apparatus, not a protective device. Commissioning shall include power-off inspection, dummy-load testing, accessible emergency stop verification, sensor zero/gain checks and single-node operation before synchronized multi-node activation.

8.16 Controlled geometry and return-channel survey

Freeze the array geometry and measurement grid before evaluating a candidate phase pattern. Survey the nominal target region and a surrounding boundary to detect redistribution of acoustic, mechanical, or other response. The selected modality determines the proper energy flux measurements; a pressure or acceleration map alone is not automatically an energy balance.

Compare at least one geometry control, one mounting or boundary-condition perturbation, phase-randomized controls, and equal-input conventional model predictions. Track source strength independently. Report where energy or response increased as well as where it decreased.

The phrase 'return channel' refers only to an instrumented pathway with a defined observable. A hypothesized pathway without a measured carrier remains an open question and must not appear as a verified item in the engineering bill of materials.

9. Experimental design, blinding, and statistical analysis

9.1 Preregistration and independent data custody

Each confirmatory experiment requires a locked protocol identifying the primary outcome, unit, direction of expected effect, experimental conditions, exclusion rules, trial count justification, randomization procedure, and statistical analysis. A party who does not control the actuators should retain the condition key until primary processing is complete. Sensor calibration and data-integrity checks may be performed without revealing condition assignments. Any exploratory frequency search or phase optimization must be labeled exploratory and repeated on a new confirmatory dataset. Complete negative and inconclusive results belong in the research record.

9.2 Analysis hierarchy

The primary analysis compares active operation with the preregistered matched sham or control condition using the defined effect statistic. Secondary analyses can explore spatial patterns, frequency dependence, response time, and differences between node configurations, with multiplicity handled explicitly. Report effect sizes and uncertainty intervals rather than relying on a p-value alone. Check residual autocorrelation and repeated-measure dependence; individual high-rate samples from one run are not independent experimental replicates. The independent experimental unit should be declared in advance, typically the run or separately reset trial rather than every sensor sample.

A preregistered test card defines the primary endpoint, measurement window, spatial grid, randomization scheme, exclusion rules, analysis model, minimum effect of interest, uncertainty threshold, and stopping rule before data collection. Exploratory analyses are labeled separately from confirmatory tests.

Blinding should separate the operator controlling the active/sham sequence from the analyst and, when practical, from the instrument operator. The condition log is retained independently, with time-stamped hashes or equivalent audit records. Sham operation must mimic relevant heat, sound, display, and control-state cues where safe and feasible.

Repeated time samples within one run are not independent experimental trials. The experimental unit should be defined at the run or randomized block level, accounting for temporal and spatial autocorrelation. Multiple frequencies, sensors, geometries, and analysis windows require a prespecified multiplicity strategy or explicit exploratory labeling.

Power analysis depends on a credible variance estimate and a minimum scientifically meaningful effect, not a convenient number of runs. Pilot data may estimate variance but should not be treated as independent confirmation of a discovered effect.

9.3 Statistical unit, estimation, and reporting

The randomized experimental run, not each sample in a high-rate waveform, is normally the unit of independent replication. If repeated observations occur within a run, use a hierarchical or blocked analysis that models run-level and day-level variability. Report the total number of runs, exclusions, missing values, and whether the primary endpoint was altered after observing data.

The primary estimand is the difference in a preregistered response statistic between active and matched sham conditions, with a confidence or credible interval and an uncertainty budget. A decibel ratio is secondary unless preregistered; its interpretation depends on whether the underlying quantity is amplitude, mean-square response, or energy. Zero or near-zero denominator conditions require a specified treatment before analysis.

Exploratory maps of phase, frequency, and geometry may generate new hypotheses. They cannot be used to claim a confirmatory effect unless a fresh held-out trial reproduces the prediction. Publication should include machine-readable data, analysis code, calibration certificates where shareable, and enough configuration metadata for an independent laboratory to repeat the work.

9.4 Laboratory scope and test article boundaries

All feasibility work shall use benign, bounded disturbances whose expected physical effects can be measured safely. The experimental article is a research instrument, not a protective product. Prototype 1A establishes a single-platform baseline; Prototype 1B investigates the six-node R1–R6 arrangement only after the baseline instrumentation and safety controls pass qualification.

Before selecting a modality, the laboratory shall state the disturbance carrier (for example acoustic pressure or low-energy mechanical vibration), propagation medium, source operating limits, spatial test volume, physical boundary conditions, and available measurement methods. A successful result in one modality cannot be generalized to impacts, radiation, or other hazards.

Define an experimental configuration identifier containing drawings, firmware versions, node serial numbers, source and sensor positions, cable routing, power limits, and environmental conditions. Any change capable of altering transfer functions requires a new configuration identifier and a documented comparability decision.

9.5 Instrumentation and metrological traceability

The measurement plan shall specify, for each channel, the physical measurand, SI unit, calibrated range, frequency response, sensitivity, resolution, sampling frequency, clock reference, and calibration date. Include the disturbance-source monitor, independent outcome sensors, electrical input measurement, temperature monitoring, and sensors for any hypothesized energy redistribution pathway.

Use a shared timebase where feasible. Where independent instruments are necessary, measure clock offsets and drift against a traceable reference at the beginning and end of each session. Characterize the full acquisition path using a known test signal, including analog filters, converter latency, and software timestamp behavior. A timestamp is not a guarantee of synchronous physical sampling.

Before each experimental block, record a zero-input noise measurement and a reference-response check. Recheck calibration after any unexpected overload, dropped data, mechanical relocation, or temperature excursion. Report instrument uncertainty separately from repeatability and model-fit uncertainty.

9.6 Spatial survey and observation geometry

Define a fixed laboratory coordinate frame and register the location and orientation of each source, actuator, receiver, support, and relevant boundary. A spatial survey shall include the nominal target region and surrounding positions where redistributed energy might appear. Record measurement-grid spacing and the physical justification for its adequacy over the selected wavelength range.

Where a dense grid is impractical, choose sampling points using a prospective coverage design rather than placing sensors only at predicted minima. For repeated surveys with a movable probe, randomize or counterbalance the order of locations and monitor the disturbance source continuously to detect drift.

Report local response maps, the spatially aggregated response index, and changes outside the nominal target volume. A localized null in a wave field may coexist with higher response elsewhere; such a pattern must be described as redistribution or interference unless further evidence supports another interpretation.

9.7 Prototype 1A commissioning sequence

Commission Prototype 1A in the following order: (i) instrument-only baseline with all actuators disabled; (ii) independently monitored disturbance-only baseline; (iii) physically present but electrically inactive hardware; (iv) sham command with no effective output; (v) bounded fixed open-loop actuation; (vi) bounded closed-loop control after stability checks; and (vii) post-run calibration and source-stability verification.

Commissioning data are for diagnosing the apparatus and estimating variance, not for confirmatory hypothesis testing. Each stage has a written entry criterion and a stop condition. No closed-loop trial proceeds if sensor saturation, unexplained source drift, actuator instability, unsafe temperatures, or unresolved electrical leakage is present.

The primary result of Prototype 1A is an experimentally characterized response and validated measurement chain. A response change consistent with known feedback or interference remains a legitimate engineering result without constituting evidence of an additional field.

9.8 Prototype 1B six-node test sequence

Characterize each of R1–R6 separately, measuring the complex transfer response to every independent receiver. Next evaluate all fifteen unordered node pairs at registered drive settings. Compare the measured pair response with the complex superposition predicted from single-node transfer functions, while monitoring total real electrical power and relevant mechanical or acoustic output.

After pairwise verification, test selected three-node groups and the full six-node configuration. Phase-pattern comparisons shall use a fixed normalization rule, fixed geometry, and independent source monitor. Include phase-randomized controls, repeated baseline conditions, and at least one geometry or mounting perturbation chosen to distinguish ordinary coupling.

Discovery trials may be used to select a candidate phase pattern. That pattern and the conventional-model prediction must then be frozen before held-out trials at new sensor positions and independent sessions. No retrospective tuning is permitted on the confirmation dataset.

9.9 Randomization, masking, and sham integrity

Generate a reproducible random assignment schedule for active, inactive, sham, and registered comparison conditions. Block or stratify by session if source or environmental drift is expected. Preserve an allocation key outside the analysis team until the preregistered primary analysis is complete.

Sham conditions must reproduce noncausal cues such as control messages, indicator states, or operator procedures without producing the active physical stimulus. Test the sham for measurable output leakage. If the active condition cannot be concealed from the operator, mask the data analyst and use automated acquisition wherever feasible.

Define and report the experimental unit correctly. Repeated samples from one physical run are not independent replicates. Separate within-run samples, repeated runs, different days, independent assemblies, and different laboratories in the statistical model.

9.10 Primary endpoint and uncertainty budget

Choose one primary endpoint before confirmatory testing. A suitable generic endpoint is the active-minus-control change in a registered spatial response index over a fixed bandwidth and region, provided its physical interpretation and units are stated. Secondary endpoints may include local spectral responses, source stability, node phase errors, electrical power, and measured redistribution channels.

Construct an uncertainty budget with instrument calibration, sensor positioning, source drift, clock synchronization, sampling and windowing, spatial quadrature, environmental variation, and between-run variability. Correlated errors shall not be treated as independent. Estimate the uncertainty of the active-control contrast using a method appropriate to the randomization and repeated-measures design.

The smallest effect of interest, confidence-interval convention, significance threshold if used, sample-size justification, and handling of missing data must be fixed prospectively. Avoid declaring a mechanism from statistical significance alone: magnitude, uncertainty, conventional-model performance, and reproducibility are separate requirements.

9.11 Data integrity and reproducible computation

Store raw sensor streams without destructive preprocessing. Preserve acquisition metadata, clock logs, calibration files, configuration hashes, condition codes, environmental measurements, and run-level operator notes. A raw-to-analysis manifest shall identify every transformation, including filtering, synchronization correction, artifact handling, spectral estimation, and spatial integration.

Freeze the analysis script or executable and its dependencies before unlocking confirmatory condition labels. Version the conventional-model parameters and specify which data were used for estimation versus validation. Maintain an audit log for excluded runs, equipment faults, protocol deviations, and reruns.

Release an independently interpretable data dictionary and a machine-readable table of trial conditions. Confidential or proprietary design details may be controlled separately, but withheld information must not prevent an independent reviewer from evaluating the scientific claim.

9.12 Failure modes and stopping rules

The independent safety controller shall disable physical actuator outputs on interlock trip, overtemperature, overcurrent, unexpected motion, electrical fault, monitoring loss or control instability. A software stop flag is not evidence of a de-energized actuator; commissioning must verify actual output current or force falls within the approved safe-state limit. The disturbance source shall return to its independently controlled baseline or shut down according to the written hazard assessment. Fault-triggered data are quarantined and never used to claim protective performance.

Pause and quarantine data when the source monitor deviates outside its preregistered tolerance, clock synchronization fails, sensors saturate, or a calibration check is unsuccessful. Record the affected trial identifiers and the reason for exclusion before viewing condition-dependent outcomes whenever practicable.

Three independent decisions are required: (1) immediate hardware safety stop under predeclared fault conditions; (2) data-validity exclusion under registered sensor, timing or calibration criteria; and (3) statistical stopping or sample-size decisions fixed before outcome inspection. An invalid run may be repeated only under a documented repeat rule and with its original record preserved. No failure to detect an effect authorizes higher actuator output or relaxed interlocks.

9.13 Independent replication and decision record

An independent replication should use a separately assembled apparatus, independently calibrated instrumentation, a frozen primary endpoint, and an analysis plan registered before data collection. The replicating team should receive the necessary physical specifications and raw-data definitions without being required to accept the proposed interpretation.

Report whether the preregistered effect was observed, whether the conventional model predicted it, whether the energy and spatial-response accounting was adequate, and whether the uncertainty permits a decisive interpretation. Use the labels supported, not supported, or inconclusive only with reference to a precisely stated experimental hypothesis.

A replicated response effect would justify further mechanism investigation, not an immediate claim of a force field or protective capability. Human exposure and hazardous-disturbance testing remain outside the present research scope.

9.14 Quantitative sensor-selection requirements

Instrument selection begins with the preregistered response quantity and the smallest effect of interest, not a preferred catalog device. For each channel define the target frequency band B, expected peak and minimum amplitudes, environmental range, required spatial resolution, and required timing alignment. The selected sensor must maintain calibrated response across B and sufficient headroom to avoid clipping in the worst credible benign test condition.

If a phase error no larger than θmax radians is allocated to timestamp uncertainty at frequency fmax, the first-order timing requirement is σt ≤ θmax/(2πfmax). For illustration only, at 200 Hz and a 1° phase allocation this yields about 13.9 microseconds. This is a timing-budget example, not a claimed CHRISTOS® device operating frequency or achieved precision. Instrument clock error, trigger uncertainty, converter skew, and mechanical propagation delays require separate allocation.

For amplitude measurements, construct a dynamic-range budget from source variability, expected attenuation, sensor self-noise, acquisition noise, and calibration drift. Choose sampling rate based on the highest signal and interference frequency requiring analysis, with an anti-alias filter and a verified passband. Merely sampling above twice the nominal excitation frequency does not guarantee adequate phase or transient fidelity.

9.15 Measurement uncertainty worked structure

Let the primary endpoint be Δ = Eactive − Econtrol, where E is a preregistered spatial response index. For paired runs, estimate the run-level differences Δk and their between-run variation. If the same source monitor, spatial calibration, or clock contributes to both conditions, the covariance must be retained: Var(Δ) = Var(Eactive) + Var(Econtrol) − 2Cov(Eactive,Econtrol).

An illustrative uncertainty ledger separates type A repeatability from type B calibration and geometry contributions. If the endpoint is computed by a nonlinear analysis pipeline, propagate uncertainty using a validated sensitivity matrix or a simulation that respects cross-channel covariance. Report sensitivity to grid density, missing sensors, window selection, and source normalization.

An uncertainty budget must have actual measured or certificate-derived numerical entries before confirmatory testing. This white paper does not invent those entries. A blank or TBD field is a commissioning requirement, not a zero-uncertainty assumption.

9.16 Trial-level schema and automated quality flags

Every trial receives a unique ID linking condition allocation, configuration hash, source calibration, sensor serial numbers, raw-file checksums, and operator log. Store both requested and measured actuator drive values; a command is not proof of delivered output. Timestamp the beginning and end of source exposure and record instrument health throughout the run.

Automated quality flags shall include saturation, dropped samples, clock discontinuity, out-of-band source drift, thermal-limit approach, actuator-command clipping, and missing redistribution-channel data. Define each flag from an instrument property or pilot characterization before examining confirmatory outcomes.

Exclude or repeat trials only under preregistered rules. Preserve excluded raw records and report sensitivity analyses including and excluding borderline records. A data-processing script must never silently replace invalid values with zeros or interpolate missing channels without recording the transformation.

9.17 Confirmatory test matrix and independence of replicates

The minimum matrix crosses disturbance-only baseline, sham, registered active pattern, randomized phase control, and at least one conventional matched-output control with the intended geometry and two qualified geometry perturbations. The final matrix depends on available power, variance, and modality. Pilot work establishes sample size; no universal number of independent runs is claimed.

Randomize condition order within each session while controlling thermal and source drift. Treat each independently initialized run as the unit for run-level inference; repeated time samples within one run improve spectral estimation but do not multiply the independent replicate count. Record sessions and assembly IDs to enable hierarchical analysis.

Confirmation requires an independent spatial receiver set and a second session or assembly. Reserve the selected phase pattern, endpoint definition, and model parameters before opening confirmation labels. A positive discovery effect that disappears on confirmation is reported as not replicated.

10. Conventional-model comparison and mechanism attribution

The default explanatory models are Maxwell-based electromagnetic coupling, acoustic wave propagation, mechanical transfer functions, thermal transport, instrument response, and control-loop dynamics. The relevant model depends on the chosen physical channel. Model parameters should be fitted on calibration or training data, then tested on held-out trials.

The proposed coherence-mediated mechanism requires more than residual noise or an unexpected active-versus-sham difference. It needs an operationally defined state variable, units, a coupling law, an independently measurable prediction, and an observation that would falsify it. Geometry and phase experiments can test whether a conventional explanation is sufficient.

A 17-second coherence-lock duration, mentioned elsewhere in the broader research program, is not accepted here as a universal physical constant. If investigated, it must be framed as a preregistered timing hypothesis with a defined onset criterion and appropriate negative controls.

10.1 Mechanism attribution: necessary but insufficient evidence

A statistically detectable response difference establishes only that the measured system differed under the tested conditions. It does not establish the cause. Mechanism attribution requires agreement with a quantitative forward model, predictive success under changed geometry or boundary conditions, and elimination of ordinary transfer pathways at an uncertainty level appropriate to the claimed effect.

For example, a phase-dependent decrease in sensor response may be produced by destructive interference at the sensor rather than a reduction in total disturbance energy. A full spatial measurement grid and, when relevant, calorimetry or distributed energy sensing are needed to distinguish local redistribution from net dissipation or redirection. Similarly, a mechanical reduction at one mount can reflect altered coupling to another mount.

The white paper therefore uses “candidate response modulation” for any observed active-versus-control difference until mechanism attribution is justified. “Non-barrier protection” remains a research objective, not a tested device capability.

10.2 Predictive signatures required for mechanism attribution

A mechanism-specific signature must do more than demonstrate that active actuation changes a sensor reading. Candidate signatures include: a phase-dependent spatial response that differs quantitatively from a calibrated linear and nonlinear model; persistence of the result when sensor and actuator cables are rerouted; reproducibility under node permutations and independent instruments; and an out-of-sample geometric prediction. These are candidate discriminators, not outcomes already observed.

A claim of selective coupling additionally requires defining which physical interaction is reduced, what remains unaffected, and how energy and momentum are redistributed. The current research cannot claim immunity to projectiles, impacts, or other hazards. Human or animal exposure, wearable deployment, and hazardous disturbance tests are outside the verification scope of this white paper.

10.3 Hierarchy of conventional forward models

Mechanism attribution shall begin with a hierarchy of conventional models of increasing fidelity, not a comparison against an intentionally weak null. Model M0 represents the calibrated disturbance-only response. M1 adds the measured linear actuator-to-sensor transfer matrix and superposition. M2 includes known cross-coupling, boundary reflections, source drift, actuator nonlinearity, and timing effects. M3, where warranted, is a modality-appropriate numerical field or structural model using measured material properties and boundary conditions.

Every model shall declare its inputs, outputs, units, fit parameters, and calibration data. Models are evaluated on held-out trials and spatial positions. The simplest model that predicts the observations within combined uncertainty remains a viable explanation; increasing complexity is justified only by predictive improvement and physical plausibility.

The proposed return-flow mechanism is not inserted as a free residual correction. Any additional term requires a prior functional form, constrained parameters, and a new prediction that cannot be generated by ordinary propagation, feedback, dissipation, or interference within reasonable parameter uncertainty.

10.4 Nested-model and predictive-score comparison

For a registered primary response vector y and model prediction μm, define the standardized residual rm = y − μm. Where the covariance matrix Σ is calibrated and invertible, a candidate discrepancy score is Qm = rmᵀΣ⁻¹rm. Its statistical interpretation depends on noise assumptions, parameter estimation, and dependence among observations; it must not be assigned a generic significance threshold without validation.

Compare out-of-sample prediction error and interval coverage across M0–M3. A model that fits the training data better but fails held-out measurements is not stronger evidence. Report model parameter counts, regularization, training/validation splits, and sensitivity to uncertain boundary conditions.

If an extended model is considered, the laboratory shall preregister its unique quantitative prediction, the minimum practically relevant effect, and the decision threshold. A favorable score alone is insufficient if the extension lacks independent physical meaning or if the same result can be achieved by a conventional model with justified parameters.

10.5 Geometry and phase discriminators

A candidate return-flow account should specify before measurement how a response feature changes when node radius, orientation, spacing, phase pattern, or support boundary changes. A useful discriminator is a signed or scaled relationship with a predicted direction and uncertainty, not merely the assertion that a change will occur.

Ordinary wave interference predicts phase-sensitive spatial maxima and minima, while structural and acoustic coupling depend on geometry and boundary impedance. Therefore geometry and phase sensitivity are expected in conventional systems. A testable additional mechanism must predict something more restrictive than phase dependence alone.

Select control geometries that are manufacturable, mechanically comparable, and matched for source input and measurement coverage. Record deviations in mass, stiffness, cable routing, and resonant response. If those differences cannot be bounded, the geometry comparison is not a clean mechanism test.

10.6 Blind challenge set and adversarial controls

An independent reviewer should prepare a blind challenge set containing ordinary phase interference, deliberate source-amplitude drift, timing offsets, sensor pickup, actuator clipping, and altered boundary conditions. The analysis team must identify which trials are adequately explained by the conventional model without knowing the injected condition.

Include negative controls in which no physical actuation occurs, and positive controls where a conventional measurable response change is expected. The goal is to verify the analysis pipeline's ability to detect known effects and reject false mechanism claims.

A candidate unexplained residual should be subjected to instrument swaps, cable rerouting, alternative receivers, changed mounting, and independent acquisition. Disappearance under one of these interventions is diagnostic evidence about the apparatus, not an inconvenience to be filtered out.

10.7 Mechanism decision table and publication threshold

Classify each registered result along two axes: reproducibility of the measured effect and adequacy of conventional explanation. A repeatable effect explained by M1–M3 is a conventional engineering finding. A nonrepeatable effect is not established. A repeatable, held-out residual beyond uncertainty is an unresolved observation until an independently specified additional model predicts it.

Claims of energy removal, force-field formation, or protective performance require evidence beyond a residual: complete relevant energy and momentum accounting, a physically identified coupling pathway, independent replication, and performance tests under the actual claimed conditions. No such claim is supported by the present conceptual design.

Publish negative and ambiguous results with the same run manifest, calibration evidence, and analysis scripts as favorable outcomes. Avoid converting a failed preregistered prediction into a different post hoc hypothesis without labeling it exploratory.

11. Energy, momentum, thermal, and safety accounting

11.1 Electrical and thermal ledger

For every powered channel, measure simultaneous voltage and current with stated polarity, bandwidth, timing alignment and integration interval. The signed net electrical work is Enet = ∫Σi vi(t)ii(t)dt. Report gross supply draw and returned energy separately when bidirectional power flow is possible; do not treat net work as gross consumption. For the declared physical control volume, identify electrical, thermal, acoustic or electromagnetic, mechanical and material energy fluxes, plus the change in stored energy. Each term requires units, a measurement or estimate, uncertainty and a sign convention. Local sensor amplitude reduction is not a conservation-law measurement.

Cross-reference to Chapter 5: normalized mean-square response ratios and local phase-order statistics are not entries in the energy or momentum ledger. A claim of protective energy diversion requires an independently measured control volume with calibrated boundary fluxes, stored-energy change, heat and mechanical work, propagated uncertainty, and a predeclared closure criterion. No such protective-effect measurement is reported in this manuscript.

11.2 Momentum and unintended pathways

For a fixed control volume, mechanical momentum closure requires Δp = ∫Fexternal dt, with force components and directions defined consistently. Measure or bound reactions transmitted to mounts, enclosures, supports, air and cabling; include momentum carried across the boundary by propagating disturbances where relevant. A lower target acceleration can arise from support motion, altered source coupling or phase cancellation. Reference force sensors and accelerometers should test these explanations. Bench work excludes projectiles, impacts, human or animal exposure, and protective-use claims.

Any change in a disturbance response must be reconciled with known energy pathways. Lower amplitude at one location can represent redistribution, absorption, cancellation, shielding, or sensor artifact. It does not imply that energy has disappeared. Spatially distributed sensors and power measurements help distinguish these possibilities.

Electrical energy, stored energy, dissipated heat, mechanical work, and radiation or acoustic output are measured as applicable. A complete balance must identify which terms are directly observed, estimated, or unmeasured. Momentum transfer likewise cannot be omitted from any future claim involving object deflection or arrest.

Independent hard limits, emergency stop, electrical isolation, thermal cutoff, and exclusion of human exposure are mandatory for bench trials. A failure to detect an effect must never trigger uncontrolled escalation of actuator power.

11.3 Energy and momentum closure protocol

At the declared electrical boundary, define P(t) = Σi vi(t)ii(t), Edraw = ∫max(P(t),0)dt and Ereturn = ∫max(−P(t),0)dt, so Enet = Edraw − Ereturn over the same interval. This decomposition assumes the channels have a consistent power-flow sign convention and that the chosen boundary is documented. It does not replace channel-by-channel accounting where simultaneous import and export can cancel in the aggregate. Record storage changes, driver losses, measurement uncertainty and thermal pathways. Electrical return to a supply is conventional circuit behavior, not evidence of the proposed Christfield return-flow mechanism.

An energy ledger includes actuator dissipation, measured radiated or transferred energy, thermal storage, mechanical work, and unexplained residuals with uncertainty. A residual is not an energy source; it may reflect incomplete measurement boundaries or model error. Momentum analysis must include mounts, enclosures, air, cabling, and surrounding structures wherever relevant.

The proposed 17-second coherence-lock interval is retained as a historical research claim, not a confirmed physical timescale. A confirmatory test would define a measurable lock criterion, compare time-to-threshold distributions against sham, and report whether any concentration near 17 seconds survives randomized replication and conventional controls.

11.4 Closed-system accounting and measurement boundaries

The control-volume ledger must name every crossing: power conductors, mechanical supports, propagating acoustic or electromagnetic energy, heat transfer, airflow and the independently driven disturbance. In a common sign convention, ΔEstored = Ein − Eout + Egenerated − Econsumed, where any generation or consumption term represents a specified and measured physical conversion or source, not a residual fitted to force closure. For a closed accounting of known physical processes, internal conversions cancel when all forms of stored energy are included. List every unmeasured term explicitly and propagate uncertainty; never infer a new energy source from the unexplained residual. For mechanical channels, pair force with velocity for work and integrate external force for momentum impulse. Neither local cancellation nor a coherent phase pattern establishes global energy or momentum removal.

11.5 Safety case and excluded claims

The laboratory safety case must document credible faults, initiating events, protective functions, verification tests, and responsible sign-off. Independent protections are not replaced by software alarms. All human exposure, wearable operation, collision mitigation, ballistic protection, and large-scale dome claims remain outside the demonstrated scope. The study may investigate low-energy, nonhazardous analogues, but results cannot be extrapolated to impact protection without an independently validated scaling law and dedicated safety evidence. This separation is both an ethical design requirement and a scientific safeguard against overinterpreting early bench effects.

11.6 Safety-case structure and claim ownership

Safety claim SC-0 concerns only controlled operation of a specific benign laboratory configuration within documented electrical, mechanical, thermal, acoustic and electromagnetic limits. The evidence package shall include a hazard register, independent interlock fault-injection results, measured actuator-off response, calibration certificates, stop-event logs and signed authorization. SC-0 does not cover impact protection, projectile arrest, wearable use, human exposure or a field-mediated protective effect. Any change in source power, actuator, geometry, firmware or operating envelope requires a recorded safety re-evaluation.

SC-0 is supported by separate claims: SC-1, hazardous energies are bounded at the source; SC-2, the apparatus has independent shutdown pathways; SC-3, instrumentation detects conditions that invalidate safe operation; SC-4, foreseeable single faults do not bypass the shutdown mechanism; and SC-5, the test team can identify and recover from an abnormal state. Each claim requires a test record, calibration reference, responsible reviewer, and unresolved-evidence status.

Any claim concerning impacts, ballistic threats, harmful radiation, high-energy disturbances, wearable operation, or protection of occupied rooms is explicitly excluded from SC-0. The proposed return-flow hypothesis cannot substitute for established protective measures.

11.7 Hazard identification and failure analysis

Perform a configuration-specific hazard analysis before energizing either prototype. At minimum, examine unintended actuation, actuator saturation, controller oscillation, electrical shock, thermal accumulation, loose fixtures, acoustic exposure, moving components, loss of sensor data, clock drift, software lockup, and unrecognized coupling to neighboring equipment.

For each hazardous event, record the initiating fault, credible consequence, severity, likelihood basis where available, preventive controls, detection method, independent mitigation, residual risk, and verification test. A qualitative risk ranking may prioritize work but does not establish numerical safety without empirical evidence.

Common-cause faults deserve special attention: shared power supplies, a common timing source, software libraries, and a single emergency-stop controller may defeat apparently independent protections. The safety review shall document which barriers are physically independent.

11.8 Independent interlocks and safe-state transition

The primary controller may request shutdown, but a separately powered or fail-safe interlock shall be able to remove actuator drive without relying on the same computation that controls phase or feedback. Define the interlock response to power loss, communication interruption, sensor saturation, excess temperature, excess current, and loss of source monitoring. Confirm that an emergency stop or watchdog fault physically inhibits the actuator driver under simulated research-controller failure, not merely that a software flag changes.

The safe state for laboratory work is actuator output disabled, with the benign disturbance source stopped or returned to a documented safe baseline. Specify the maximum permitted shutdown delay from measured fault detection to verified energy removal; the numerical limit must be derived from the actual hardware hazard assessment rather than chosen by analogy.

Validate the safe state by fault injection during low-energy commissioning, recording physical output and stored-energy decay. Emergency-stop buttons, protective earth, and current limits shall be evaluated against applicable laboratory electrical practice; none is assumed effective merely because it appears on a schematic.

11.9 Fault-injection and recovery verification

A bounded fault-injection plan should include disconnected sensors, corrupted or delayed timestamps, one actuator commanded incorrectly, controller reboot, network interruption, open circuit, and thermal-sensor failure where the hardware permits safe simulation. Tests must be designed so that the injected fault cannot create an uncontrolled hazard.

For each injected fault, record the detection channel, time to detection, physical output at the instant of detection, time to verified safe state, and whether recovery requires manual acknowledgement. Any unobserved output or automatic restart that violates the approved safety case blocks progression.

Restart is a new commissioning event: verify source baseline, calibration, firmware identity, and interlock function before resuming confirmatory trials. Data from the fault interval must remain traceable and cannot be silently deleted.

11.10 Exposure boundary and human exclusion

All proposed feasibility tests are non-human, non-protective experiments. The test boundary must account for ordinary physical hazards of the source and actuators, including sound pressure, motion, heating, and electrical contact. Appropriate established guards and personal protective equipment are determined by the host laboratory, not by any assumed field effect.

No volunteer, operator, or bystander may be placed in a hazardous exposure pathway to demonstrate protection. Mannequin or inert test-object studies may be considered only after separate conventional safety assessment, and cannot be interpreted as evidence of human safety.

Any future wearable or occupied-space concept requires a new design history, exposure assessment, human-factors program, and regulatory evaluation appropriate to its intended use. It is outside the present white paper’s validated scope.

11.11 Control-volume definition and energy flux ledger

Define a fixed physical control volume V surrounding the benign test apparatus, its support structure, and any intentionally instrumented redistribution pathway. Identify every source, electrical feed, mechanical attachment, thermal boundary, and wave-transmission boundary. A local response reduction at one receiver is not a control-volume energy balance.

For a control volume V and an explicitly defined energy inventory, write dEV/dt + ∮∂V J·n dA = Pin − Pdiss, where J is the outward boundary flux, Pin is external source power entering through separately accounted channels, and Pdiss is an energy conversion excluded from the chosen inventory. If EV already includes internal thermal energy, conversion to heat inside V is not a loss term and must not be subtracted. The ledger must specify which convention is used.

Integrate over a registered observation window and report the closure residual with uncertainty. A statistically unresolved closure residual cannot be interpreted as energy disappearing into a singularity-like center. The proposed organizing center remains a conceptual hypothesis until a measurable carrier and transfer law are established.

11.12 Electrical input and heat measurement protocol

Measure real electrical power at the relevant input boundary using synchronized voltage and current waveforms: Preal = mean[v(t)i(t)] over a stated interval. Apparent power, command amplitude, and supply nameplate rating are not substitutes for real input power. Report instrument bandwidth, phase calibration, sampling method, and uncertainty.

Measure component and ambient temperatures at documented locations and characterize steady-state and transient heat behavior. Where calorimetry is feasible, state the thermal mass, heat capacity, insulation assumptions, calibration method, and heat-transfer pathways. Electrical energy converted into heat must be included rather than treated as an unexplained loss.

Record input power for each individual node and the complete array. The difference between the sum of isolated-node power and simultaneous-array power can reflect electrical or mechanical coupling and must be characterized before equal-energy phase claims are made.

11.13 Momentum and reaction-force accounting

A proposed reduction in transmitted mechanical disturbance must be evaluated against forces and impulses at supports and other boundaries. Measure relevant reaction forces, accelerations, and momentum transfer using a geometry appropriate to the selected benign modality. A reduction at one point can arise from changed wave paths or increased loading elsewhere.

For a control volume, the time rate of change of momentum equals the net applied external force plus the appropriate momentum-flux terms. A useful experimental ledger records source impulse, transmitted response, support reactions, and known dissipative effects over the same observation window.

If the instrumentation cannot resolve all relevant reaction pathways, report the momentum ledger as partial. Do not infer cancellation of momentum from a single low-response receiver.

11.14 Propagated uncertainty and closure acceptance

Assign standard uncertainties to input-power calibration, source monitoring, spatial flux estimation, support-force measurements, thermal estimates, sensor positions, and timing alignment. Preserve covariance when the same calibration reference or clock affects multiple channels. The combined closure uncertainty shall be computed from the specified measurement model, not by adding all errors as though independent.

Select the acceptable closure tolerance prospectively using the smallest effect of interest and achievable instrument sensitivity. A measured residual smaller than combined uncertainty is unresolved; a residual larger than uncertainty triggers calibration and conventional-model investigation before any new-mechanism interpretation.

Include sensitivity analysis for unmeasured boundaries and uncertain material parameters. Where these dominate the ledger, the appropriate conclusion is that the apparatus does not yet support decisive energy accounting.

Each proposed physical claim shall map to a requirement identifier, an observable, an SI unit or clearly labeled dimensionless index, a sensor or instrument, a calibration method, a registered test, a conventional prediction, a decision rule, and a falsifying outcome. Example RQ-M01 concerns six-node superposition: measure the complex receiver response from each isolated node, predict pairwise response by complex addition, and compare against measured pairwise response using calibrated uncertainty. Acceptance review shall sample raw records and reproduce at least one calibration calculation and one response-index calculation without relying on the primary analyst's exported figures. Discrepancies must be resolved or recorded before release.

The review conclusion must separate equipment readiness, data integrity, measured effect, mechanism attribution, and protective performance. These are different evidentiary claims with different acceptance standards.

12.7 Instrument acceptance record and numerical gates

For each actuator, acceptance requires verified command-to-output behavior, input-power characterization, duty limits, and bounded response to fault conditions. The acceptance record includes the measured value, uncertainty, instrument identifier, procedure version, witness, and disposition.

Numerical thresholds must be derived from the target signal and uncertainty allocation. A tolerance that has not been justified by an equipment rating, calibration, safety analysis, or prospective sensitivity study remains TBD. A test that passes only because a tolerance was relaxed after inspecting its result is not a valid acceptance test.

Failed acceptance tests trigger fault isolation, corrective action, and a new configuration revision. The original failure and all associated raw data remain in the record.

13. Evidence levels and decision framework

13.1 Evidence thresholds and publication language

Level E0 documents a concept and mathematical proposal. Level E1 demonstrates calibrated and reproducible instrumentation. Level E2 establishes a controlled change in a specified measurable response. Level E3 demonstrates a repeatable effect not adequately explained by preregistered conventional models, with independent replication. Level E4 would require application-specific safety and performance evidence under the intended operating conditions. Progress at one level does not automatically establish the next. The present white paper documents E0-level research design and engineering specifications; it does not report independently verified E2–E4 results. All public claims should identify the evidence level actually supported.

Evidence should be evaluated in stages. Stage E0 is a documented design. E1 is a functioning instrument. E2 is a repeatable measured response under controlled conditions. E3 is a result that survives conventional-model and artifact challenges. E4 is independent replication of a specified mechanistic prediction. E5, if ever reached, would be a separate application-specific safety and protective-performance qualification.

A statistically detectable response is not equivalent to a useful response, and neither is equivalent to demonstrated protection. The manuscript should report effect size, uncertainty, absolute baseline, spatial distribution, energy input, and conditions of failure. Null and inconclusive results are scientifically valuable because they constrain the hypothesis.

13.2 Negative, null, and ambiguous outcomes

A null outcome occurs when the prespecified effect is not detected with adequate sensitivity. It should be reported with the achievable uncertainty and effect-size bounds rather than called a failed experiment. An inconclusive outcome occurs when instrumentation, statistical power, or confounds prevent a meaningful test of the hypothesis. A negative result may exclude a particular parameter range without excluding every possible implementation.

A positive active-versus-sham result should trigger an adversarial replication plan: change operators, re-route cables, swap sensors, reverse the physical arrangement, independently recalibrate instruments, and repeat with an unseen randomized sequence. Only after that replication should the research claim advance from a preliminary signal to a robust measured phenomenon.

13.3 Claim-to-evidence audit protocol

Every scientific statement in the manuscript shall be tagged as conceptual hypothesis (H), conventional physical relation (C), engineering design requirement (D), simulation or analytic prediction (P), experimental observation (O), or independently replicated observation (R). A single paragraph may contain multiple classes, but each factual assertion must retain its own evidence class. No assertion should migrate from H or P to O merely because it is expressed in mathematical notation.

The claim ledger shall include a stable identifier, exact claim wording, location in the manuscript, observable and units if applicable, supporting source or dataset, model assumptions, counterhypothesis, verification status, and permitted publication language. Missing source evidence shall be recorded as a gap, not filled by inference.

Examples: C-001, linear superposition under the linear time-invariant approximation, is a conventional model assumption requiring regime verification; H-001, a structured inward return pathway toward a singularity-like organizing center, is the originating research hypothesis; D-001, independent safety shutdown, is a design requirement; O-001 must remain unused until an actual instrumented experiment produces traceable data.

13.4 Evidence independence and circularity checks

A measured decrease at a selected sensor cannot simultaneously serve as the definition of a return pathway, the evidence that the pathway exists, and the independent test of the pathway. Such reuse is circular. Separate the mechanism proposal, the predicted signature, the measured endpoint, and the independent physical accounting.

The same calibration data must not be counted as independent model confirmation. Similarly, multiple spectral bins or time samples within one run do not constitute multiple independent physical replicates. A replication claim requires separately collected data and a predeclared comparison against the frozen prediction.

Where an effect is obtained only after optimizing many phase patterns, report the discovery search and reserve held-out runs for confirmation. A residual identified after reviewing data is exploratory until it yields a new prospective prediction.

13.5 Alternative-explanation closure register

Maintain a closure register for conventional alternatives including destructive interference, boundary reflection, actuator back-action, mechanical loading, thermal drift, source instability, electrical pickup, digitizer saturation, clock misalignment, and analysis selection. For each alternative, identify the relevant test, expected signature, uncertainty, and whether the test has actually been performed.

An alternative is not ruled out by an assertion that the geometry is unusual. The closure record should identify a measurement that would discriminate the candidate explanation, including an instrument swap, geometry control, cable rerouting, source monitor, or independently sampled boundary flux.

Unresolved alternatives must accompany the published result. A laboratory may legitimately report a repeatable phenomenon with unresolved mechanism, provided the evidence and limitations are stated accurately.

13.6 Quantitative decision logic for anomalous residuals

For each held-out trial, define r = ymeasured − yconventional, with a documented covariance model Σ for measurement and prediction uncertainty. The acceptance region must be established before inspection of confirmation data and should account for correlated channels, parameter estimation, and multiple planned tests.

A residual outside a registered uncertainty region initiates a staged investigation: first recheck calibration and raw-data integrity; second challenge known conventional coupling and nonlinear models; third repeat on the same apparatus with randomized controls; fourth replicate on an independently assembled instrument; fifth test a separately specified physical prediction. No single step alone establishes an additional mechanism.

Report effect size, uncertainty interval, model coverage, and sensitivity to reasonable alternative specifications. If the result is not repeatable or becomes consistent with conventional predictions after correcting an error, record that outcome rather than reclassifying it as hidden protection.

14. Manufacturing readiness and configuration control

The manufacturing path follows staged design reviews: M0 research configuration freeze; M1 preliminary design review; M2 detailed design review; M3 bench commissioning; M4 experimental qualification; and M5 replication decision. These are decision gates, not assertions that fabrication-ready drawings exist.

A bill of materials, wiring diagrams, interface control documents, thermal and electrical limits, firmware hashes, calibration certificates, inspection records, and revision-controlled build instructions are needed before any released laboratory build. The five figures in this white paper are conceptual and cannot substitute for those documents.

14.1 Design freeze, bill of materials, and reproducible assembly

Before independent testing, freeze a controlled bill of materials, electrical schematic, mechanical drawings, cable and grounding diagram, firmware hash, acquisition configuration, calibration list, and safety assessment. All changes after freeze receive a change identifier and an impact analysis stating whether prior results remain comparable.

An assembly traveler records inspection steps and as-built deviations. Component substitutions, particularly resonators, coils, sensors, amplifiers, and clock modules, require requalification because small differences can alter conventional coupling and timing. Engineering drawings must label conceptual elements that have not yet been physically realized.

14.2 Minimum reproducible laboratory build record

A replicating laboratory needs a controlled drawing index, bill of materials with acceptable substitutions, as-built dimensional inspection, wiring and grounding diagram, instrument calibration files, firmware and analysis-code hashes, parameter set, and commissioning checklist. The build record must distinguish specified properties from measured properties. If a material or oscillator is substituted, requalification is required for any characteristic that could affect phase, transfer response, or measurement leakage. The five conceptual figures aid comprehension but do not establish tolerances, component ratings, or manufacturing readiness.

14.3 Independent replication package

Release a preregistered protocol and an anonymized condition code before confirmatory testing. At completion, publish the raw-data schema, calibrated instrument metadata, processing pipeline, exclusions, and signed deviations from protocol, subject to appropriate intellectual-property boundaries. A second laboratory should be able to reproduce the experiment without relying on undocumented tuning by the originating team. Independent replication is evaluated against the registered primary endpoint, not by selecting a different favorable metric after results are known. Negative replication is informative and should be retained in the evidence record.

14.4 Reproducible build dossier and controlled substitutions

A reproducible build dossier shall include a versioned bill of materials, approved substitutes, mechanical drawings, circuit schematics, wiring and grounding diagrams, firmware binaries and hashes, calibration procedures, inspection forms, and a commissioning checklist. Photographs may support but not replace dimensional or electrical specifications.

Any component substitution shall be evaluated for changes to frequency response, phase delay, output impedance, heating, geometry, and electromagnetic compatibility. A substitution that alters a critical transfer function requires re-characterization and may invalidate prior confirmation data.

The dossier shall separate information needed to reproduce the scientific test from proprietary material that is not essential to evaluate the reported result. A claim of independent reproducibility requires access to sufficient implementation detail.

14.5 Laboratory transfer and release decision

Before transfer to an external laboratory, provide the approved research scope, hazard analysis, safety interlock description, acceptance tests, source and sensor calibration records, configuration manifest, frozen analysis plan, and raw-data dictionary. Include a list of unresolved scientific and engineering questions in the transfer package.

The receiving laboratory should conduct its own safety and feasibility review and may decline or modify the protocol. Any protocol change shall be documented before data collection, with clear separation of exploratory and confirmatory runs.

14.14 Dimensional and uncertainty worksheet for phase-sensitive tests

Let fmax denote the highest frequency at which the registered phase comparison is evaluated, and let σt be the standard uncertainty of the relative timing reference. For a pure delay approximation, σ_φ ≈ 2π fmax σt, in radians. At 200 Hz, a one-degree phase uncertainty corresponds to approximately 13.9 microseconds; this is an illustrative calculation rather than an equipment specification.

The total phase budget must include sampling-clock error, trigger skew, analog group-delay differences, cable and driver delay, actuator phase response, propagation-path uncertainty, and estimation variance. These components may be correlated. Report the measured combined uncertainty rather than assuming that a shared clock eliminates all phase errors.

Where the physical wavelength is comparable to geometric positioning uncertainty, include position and orientation errors in the predicted phase and amplitude response. A phase-sensitive cancellation claim that ignores spatial positioning can produce artificially narrow confidence intervals.

14.15 Verification protocol for spatial response maps

Before testing, fix the coordinate system, source position, array geometry, measurement region, surrounding guard region, frequency band, and grid-selection rule. Record both the local minimum response and a spatially aggregated endpoint. Report guard-region increases as well as target-region decreases to reveal redistribution.

Acquire the disturbance-only, inactive-hardware, sham, active-fixed-phase, and phase-randomized conditions in randomized or counterbalanced order. Use a continuous independent source monitor and environmental record. Repeat baseline measurements throughout the session so that drift can be estimated without assuming stationarity.

Keep the discovery grid and confirmatory grid separate. The chosen phase configuration and prediction must be frozen before confirmatory measurements. If the effect appears only at optimized positions and fails the held-out grid, classify it as a discovery observation rather than a validated spatial response.

15. Scientific limitations and responsible claims

No current evidence supplied in the integrated master demonstrates a selective non-barrier protective field, a novel physical force, a universal disturbance-decoupling mechanism, or protection from high-energy objects. This white paper does not propose live threat testing or protective deployment.

Even a strong conventional acoustic or vibration-control result would establish only performance within that tested medium, geometry, frequency band, energy level, and environmental condition. Claims must not be generalized across physical categories without new experiments.

The paper is therefore appropriate for theoretical critique, bench-test planning, and independent research discussion. It is not a certification, product safety dossier, or protective equipment specification.

15.1 Explicit limits of the current evidence

This white paper does not report independently replicated laboratory evidence of selective disturbance decoupling, force-field protection, energy removal through a return pathway, or safe use around people. The equations are principally measurement definitions and candidate models; they do not by themselves derive a new interaction from established field theory.

Any future use of terms such as “shield,” “deflection,” or “protection” should be accompanied by the relevant tested load, operating conditions, failure rate, uncertainty, and independent verification. Until those data exist, the responsible description is a proposed adaptive interaction-envelope research architecture.

15.2 Dimensional-consistency and terminology review

Use energy (joules), power (watts), force (newtons), impulse (newton-seconds), acceleration (meters per second squared), pressure (pascals), and phase (radians or degrees) only when the measured quantity warrants those units. A spatial index formed from squared sensor amplitudes must not be called energy without a calibrated physical conversion and integration rule.

The term coherence shall identify a specific estimator or operational measure, such as magnitude-squared coherence, and its data-processing assumptions. A hypothesized physical coherence field requires independent operational definition, SI dimensions, coupling, and predictions. These two uses must not be conflated.

The phrase singularity-like center refers to the proposed organizing role in the originating hypothesis, not a literal astrophysical singularity or black hole. Toroidal organization remains a candidate geometry rather than a necessary premise. The six-node apparatus is a proposed test implementation, not the definition of the hypothesis.

15.3 Boundary of protective performance claims

No benchtop demonstration of wave cancellation or vibration attenuation licenses a claim of protection against dangerous impacts, weapons, radiation, explosions, or human exposure. These domains involve different physical carriers, safety standards, time scales, energies, and failure modes.

For any future application, performance claims would require a clearly defined threat or disturbance, validated operating envelope, failure probabilities, independent safety testing, and appropriate regulatory review. The present work is restricted to safe, bounded laboratory phenomena.

The research program explicitly excludes weaponization, harmful exposure testing, and demonstrations that could place people at risk based on an unverified effect.

16. Research program and milestones

16.1 Research decisions after the first laboratory campaign

A first laboratory campaign should produce one of three outcomes: a reproducible controlled effect with quantified uncertainty; an effect explainable by conventional coupling or artifact; or an inconclusive result requiring better measurement. Each outcome has scientific value. The subsequent decision is not simply whether to scale the apparatus. It is whether a specific hypothesis survived a specific test, whether the measurement system is adequate, and whether an independent laboratory can repeat the observation. Engineering scale-up remains conditional on safety, power accounting, and replicated evidence, not on the ambition of the eventual application.

Phase I: select one benign measurand, finalize the safety case, and qualify instruments. Phase II: commission Prototype 1A and preregister randomized trials. Phase III: test spatial and phase dependence against conventional baselines. Phase IV: qualify Prototype 1B nodes and complete controlled six-node experiments. Phase V: independently reproduce a specific positive result or formally document the null result.

A transition from exploratory research to a mechanism claim requires preregistered quantitative predictions and a result not adequately explained by conventional models. Any later application program would need its own requirements, hazard analysis, and regulatory pathway.

16.2 Phased independent research campaign

Phase I documents the conventional baseline and qualifies the instrumentation. Deliverables are the frozen test card, calibrated sensor inventory, safety review, and raw inactive/sham reference data. Phase II characterizes single-node and six-node behavior under matched energy conditions, producing response maps, phase records, and a conventional-model residual analysis.

Phase III repeats the strongest preregistered prediction under independent supervision with a held-out randomization sequence. Phase IV, contingent on reproducible results, develops and tests a physically explicit mechanism model. Only a separate safety and regulatory program could later consider any protective application. Progression is evidence-gated rather than calendar-gated.

16.3 Scientific consistency audit gates

Gate A, architecture integrity: confirm that all descriptions preserve the inward return-flow hypothesis and do not silently replace it with a golden-ratio lattice, toroidal requirement, or new-force assertion. Gate B, mathematics: verify every symbol, unit, boundary condition, and assumption; identify expressions used only as bookkeeping identities.

Gate C, evidence: inspect every claimed observation for a traceable dataset, calibrated measurement, analysis record, and independent status. Gate D, experiment: check whether primary endpoints, controls, randomization, uncertainty, and stopping rules are specified prospectively. Gate E, safety: confirm that no proposed trial relies on unverified protective effectiveness.

Gate F, publication: reconcile section numbering, figure captions, cross-references, table definitions, terminology, provenance, and evidence-language consistency. Each gate requires a named reviewer and a written disposition of pass, conditional, or open; a narrative declaration of completion is insufficient.

16.4 Independent review questions and decision outputs

Independent reviewers should be asked: Which mathematical expressions are definitions rather than derived laws? Which measurements are capable of distinguishing ordinary interference from the proposed mechanism? Are all relevant energy and momentum pathways instrumented? Does the uncertainty budget resolve the smallest effect of interest? Can the apparatus be reconstructed from the design package?

The reviewer record should separate fatal scientific blockers, fixable engineering gaps, and editorial issues. An unresolved physical mechanism is not automatically a reason to prohibit a safe feasibility pilot, provided the pilot tests a bounded, falsifiable measurement question without unsupported performance claims.

The final review output is a decision matrix: proceed with documentation, proceed with benign feasibility work, revise the protocol, or defer experimentation pending measurement and safety corrections. The decision must name the specific evidence needed to advance.

17. Conclusions

The CHRISTOS® non-barrier concept is most productively framed as a research program in adaptive, spatially distributed disturbance interaction. Its near-term value lies in making a broad hypothesis testable through calibrated sensing, bounded actuation, phase-aware control, independent verification, and falsification.

The proposed system’s scientific credibility will depend on controlled data rather than on the elegance of its diagrams or the scale of its supporting manuscript. A negative result would narrow the feasible coupling mechanisms. A positive result would invite independent replication and careful attribution. Neither outcome justifies bypassing energy accounting or safety requirements.

The next legitimate milestone is a documented, independently reviewable benign bench experiment, not a claim of demonstrated protection.

Appendix G. Laboratory Feasibility Protocol: Controlled Benign Disturbance Study

Status: proposed protocol for qualified laboratory review. No apparatus is represented as built, calibrated, approved, or demonstrated. The following procedures define a documentation and measurement framework rather than permission to conduct hazardous testing.

G.1 Purpose, research questions, and scope

Primary feasibility question: Can a spatially distributed, instrumented, bounded actuator arrangement modify a benign physical disturbance within a prespecified region, and can the result be quantitatively predicted using conventional models? The first endpoint concerns reproducible physical response, not a claimed new force or protective effect.

Secondary questions: Does a six-node array produce phase-dependent spatial response consistent with a calibrated transfer model? Are changes attributable to source drift, interference, support coupling, feedback, or dissipation? Can the measurement chain resolve a preregistered effect while independently accounting for input and redistribution channels?

Exploratory question: Is there a reproducible held-out residual that persists after the registered conventional-model and uncertainty challenges? Any additional physical interpretation requires an independently specified mathematical law and a new prospective prediction.

G.2 Selection of benign test modality

The laboratory shall choose one low-energy modality for the first campaign, such as a safely bounded acoustic field or low-amplitude mechanical vibration. This document does not assume that results in one carrier generalize to electromagnetic exposure, impacts, heat, radiation, or human safety.

The modality selection memo shall define the source, propagation medium, receiver type, boundary conditions, maximum permitted operating envelope, spatial scale, wavelength range, source monitoring, and expected environmental confounders. Record why the chosen modality is safe, measurable, and compatible with a useful conventional forward model.

Do not mix modalities within the primary confirmatory endpoint. A second modality requires a separate protocol, calibration chain, physical model, uncertainty budget, and preregistered claim.

G.3 Laboratory configuration and coordinate survey

Define the laboratory frame with an origin, axis directions, and survey reference markers. Register the source location, all six node centers and orientations, sensor locations, support contacts, cables, and the nearest reflective or compliant boundaries. Record environmental conditions relevant to the selected medium.

Each physical configuration receives a unique identifier. Changes in node mounting, source position, cable routing, support stiffness, specimen geometry, or firmware require an assessment of whether prior transfer measurements remain valid. Use photographs and dimensioned drawings as complementary records; photographs alone are not sufficient for dimensional reconstruction.

The spatial survey shall include a primary target region, a guard region for possible redistribution, and reference points near source and support boundaries. Grid spacing must be justified relative to the smallest wavelength and expected spatial gradients.

G.4 Instrument acceptance and reference measurements

For each channel record manufacturer or design identifier, serial number, measurand, unit, calibrated bandwidth, sensitivity, uncertainty, noise floor, dynamic range, phase response, sampling rate, and clock source. An independent source monitor and at least one independent outcome recorder are required for interpretability.

Before any active trial, acquire an instrument-only noise record, a known reference response, and a synchronization check. Quantify timing skew, channel delay, leakage, clipping, and data loss. Repeat the reference checks after apparatus changes and at the end of each session.

Acceptance values shall be established from equipment ratings and the required measurement sensitivity. The protocol intentionally leaves numerical pass limits open until a qualified laboratory selects the hardware and test carrier.

G.5 Conventional model M0: disturbance-only baseline

With all actuators physically present but disabled, characterize the disturbance-only field across the registered region. Measure source output continuously and repeat baseline acquisitions to estimate short-term drift and between-run variance. Distinguish an empty test space from the physically installed but inactive apparatus, because passive hardware can alter the field.

Build M0 as the registered baseline prediction conditioned on measured source strength and relevant environment. Record spectral density, phase where measurable, and spatial response. Reserve a subset of baseline trials for validation rather than fitting every observed variation.

The laboratory shall document the limitations of M0 before active experiments. A weak baseline is not evidence of an unconventional interaction.

G.6 Conventional model M1: individual node transfer functions

For each node i in R1–R6, apply a safely bounded and independently monitored input while the remaining nodes are inactive. Measure complex transfer responses Hji(f) at each receiver j and frequency f. Record actuator real electrical input and the physically relevant output proxy or direct output measurement.

Repeat the characterization under the same registered geometry and environmental envelope. Quantify phase and magnitude repeatability, frequency-dependent latency, and cross-channel pickup. If the plant is nonlinear over the proposed operating range, the linear model is valid only locally and the permitted range must be restricted or a higher-order model adopted.

Do not use the isolated-node data as an independent confirmation of a six-node effect. Their purpose is to estimate the conventional model that will predict later combined-node trials.

G.7 Pairwise model verification

There are fifteen unordered pairs among six nodes. For each pair, predict the complex receiver response by superposing the separately measured node contributions with the disturbance response. Compare that prediction with the measured pair response at the registered receiver locations and input normalization.

Pairwise residuals shall be inspected for systematic phase error, actuator loading, support-mediated coupling, saturation, thermal drift, and source variation. A residual may require an updated conventional model, but it is not automatically a return-flow signature.

Randomize or counterbalance pair order and repeat the baseline at defined intervals. Report all fifteen pair outcomes, not only pairs with the strongest reduction.

G.8 Full-array phase study

Register a finite set of phase patterns, including at least one conventional predicted optimum, a uniform-phase reference, and phase-randomized controls. Specify whether the comparison fixes real electrical input, measured physical output, or another normalization; report the quantities that vary.

Separate phase-pattern discovery from confirmation. The candidate pattern may be chosen using a discovery grid, but the final test must use frozen settings and held-out sensor positions or sessions. Avoid retuning based on confirmatory data.

For each pattern report source-normalized local response, spatial aggregate, guard-region response, total electrical input, timing error, and safety state. A local reduction accompanied by a guard-region increase is evidence of redistribution, not energy removal.

G.9 Sham and adversarial control sequence

Sham conditions shall reproduce the controller commands and operator cues without delivering the active physical output. Test for residual leakage from electronics and indicators. Additional controls shall include inactive hardware, an alternate mounting configuration, source-amplitude perturbations within safe limits, and an instrument swap where practical.

Introduce known synthetic timing offsets or source drift in a blind analysis challenge set, without compromising hardware safety. The analysis pipeline should identify or reject these conventional causes before any claim of unexplained behavior.

The blind allocation record must remain separate from the primary analyst until the registered processing steps are complete. Record all protocol deviations before examining condition-specific outcome differences whenever possible.

G.10 Data acquisition and integrity rules

Preserve raw waveforms and event logs, including source monitor, independent receivers, node drive channels, real electrical input, environmental sensors, fault events, and timing reference. Every file shall carry a configuration ID, trial ID, condition code, instrument IDs, acquisition time, and a cryptographic hash.

Declare filtering, window length, overlap, spectral estimator, interpolation, and outlier rules before confirmatory processing. Never replace raw data with a smoothed export. Document missing samples, saturation, or clock slips with trial-level flags.

The analysis software shall produce the same endpoint from the same raw dataset under a frozen version. A reviewer should be able to reconstruct at least one result from the archived inputs.

G.11 Primary endpoint and analysis

A candidate primary endpoint is the active-minus-control change in a prespecified spatial response index, integrated over a registered frequency band and target region. The index must declare its units and physical interpretation. A sum of squared sensor amplitudes is not automatically energy.

Use the randomization unit, not the number of digital samples, to define independent replication. Estimate uncertainty using a method that respects within-session correlation and between-session variation. Report effect size, uncertainty interval, and the registered decision rule.

Model comparison must use held-out predictions. An additional mechanism is not supported merely because a flexible fitted model reduces residual error. A specific alternative must have constrained parameters and a novel prospective prediction.

G.12 Energy and momentum accounting

Define a modality-appropriate control volume, time interval, sign convention, and boundary ledger. Measure relevant source input, actuator input, storage, transmitted flux, support reaction, and dissipation or thermal pathways. Explicitly identify any boundary not instrumented.

For a conventional energy ledger, the closure residual is measured inputs minus the sum of measured storage change, outflows, and justified internal conversion terms. Report its uncertainty and sensitivity to missing pathways. A nonzero closure residual may indicate incomplete metrology or modelling.

The proposed inward return-flow pathway cannot be inserted into the ledger as an observed energy sink without a physical carrier, measured flux, and receiving boundary.

G.13 Stop-work, safety, and fault handling

The laboratory hazard assessment shall specify approved source and actuator limits, mechanical restraints, electrical isolation, temperature monitoring, and independent emergency shutdown. Research software cannot be the sole means of limiting hazardous output.

Stop work upon any interlock trip, unanticipated motion, excessive temperature or current, sensor saturation that defeats monitoring, loss of source control, or unresolved timing and acquisition faults. Quarantine affected runs and document the event.

This protocol excludes human exposure and hazardous disturbance demonstrations. A benign laboratory result cannot be presented as proof of protection in dangerous environments.

G.14 Replication and reporting disposition

An independent team should receive the frozen design, model specification, calibration procedure, primary endpoint, analysis scripts, and raw-data dictionary. It should be able to assemble and characterize its own apparatus without relying on undocumented decisions.

Report five separate outcomes: apparatus readiness; reproducibility of the measured effect; predictive adequacy of conventional models; energy and momentum accounting completeness; and whether a prespecified additional model makes and survives a unique prediction.

Allowed conclusions are limited to the evidence. A successful conventional phase-control result is scientifically useful. A reproducible unresolved residual justifies further study. Neither outcome alone validates a protective field.

Appendix J. Engineering Model, Dimensional Definitions, and Prediction Gates

This appendix formalizes candidate mathematical descriptions for a bounded, benign disturbance-control study. These equations are conventional reference models unless explicitly labelled as a hypothesis. None is a derived law of Christfield Dynamics, and none establishes a protective field.

J.1 System boundaries and state variables

Let x(t) be a vector of physical states in a selected propagation medium, u(t) the six commanded actuator inputs, d(t) the independently monitored disturbance, and y(t) the measured outputs. A local linearized reference model can be written dx/dt = A x + B u + E d + w; y = C x + D u + F d + v. The matrices depend on the physical carrier, geometry, and operating point. Process and measurement errors w and v are not presumed independent until tested.

The state vector cannot be assigned universal units. Each component must be declared in an engineering model register, for example displacement (m), velocity (m/s), pressure (Pa), or circuit current (A). Every matrix entry inherits units from the states and inputs it maps. A numerical state-space fit with undocumented scaling is not a physically interpretable model.

The model must include the source-to-receiver path and actuator-to-receiver paths. A controller that merely suppresses a measured signal at one location is not evidence that the disturbance has been eliminated from the control volume.

J.2 Frequency-domain six-node transfer representation

At a fixed angular frequency ω, define the complex receiver vector Y(ω) = Hd(ω)D(ω) + Hu(ω)U(ω) + N(ω), where Hd maps the monitored disturbance into receivers and Hu is an m-by-six matrix of actuator-to-receiver transfer functions. U contains the measured actuator inputs or a documented proxy, not simply nominal software commands.

If receiver channels measure acoustic pressure in pascals and actuator inputs are voltages, entries of Hu have units Pa/V. If the inputs are measured force in newtons, Hu has units Pa/N. Transfer functions estimated under one normalization cannot be compared to those under another without conversion.

For two nodes i and k, the predicted pair response is Yik = Y0 + Hi Ui + Hk Uk, under the registered linearity assumptions. Disagreement can indicate coupling, nonlinear loading, drift, phase error, or incorrect boundaries. The discrepancy is a model-diagnostic result rather than evidence of a novel field.

J.3 Closed-loop stability and causality

For a conventional negative-feedback loop with plant P(s) and controller K(s), the sensitivity function is S(s) = [I + P(s)K(s)]^(-1), where the plus sign follows the specified negative-feedback convention. The sign must be changed if the implementation uses a different summing junction. A low magnitude of S at one frequency or receiver does not establish broadband or spatial stability.

Record sensor-to-controller latency, actuator response delay, saturation, and any adaptive update timing. The closed-loop test must establish that safety constraints are enforced independently of the research controller. A stable offline simulation is insufficient to qualify real-time operation.

Use bounded commands and a separately tested fallback state. Unexpected oscillation, excessive gain, clipping, or sensor failure must cause a safe shutdown. Stability claims shall be restricted to the tested operating envelope.

J.4 Phase, synchronization, and spatial uncertainty

Under the exp(+iωt) phasor convention, a pure positive delay τ multiplies a harmonic response by exp(−iωτ), giving a signed phase change Δφ = −2πfτ radians. The magnitude of its first-order timing uncertainty is σφ ≈ 2πf στ when timing uncertainty dominates. Transducer dispersion, filtering, propagation geometry, and calibration may contribute additional phase error. Report wrapped phase and any unwrapping procedure.

For a propagating plane wave with wave vector k and position uncertainty δr, the approximate spatial phase error is δφ = k·δr. A complete phase budget should retain correlations between surveyed positions, clock errors, and common calibration sources. A shared clock does not remove propagation-path uncertainty.

The laboratory shall specify whether phase is measured relative to the disturbance monitor, to a common actuator reference, or to an independent time base. Phase comparisons made under inconsistent references are invalid.

J.5 Registered response and attenuation metrics

Define a scalar sensor quantity z(t) in a declared SI unit and a common acquisition window T. A candidate mean-square response is M = (1/T)∫[0,T] z(t)^2 dt. The units are the square of the sensor unit; M is not automatically energy or power. The spatially aggregated endpoint must define weighting and the target region before measurement.

For matched units, calibration and analysis windows, define the illustrative response metric RdB = 10 log₁₀(Mcontrol/Mactive), where M is a positive, consistently normalized mean-square sensor response. Both M values must exceed the prespecified validated quantification floor to report a numerical ratio; otherwise give a bound or censored result. A response ratio is not a general measurement of physical energy reduction, especially if source strength, bandwidth or sensor calibration changes between conditions.

Include the corresponding source-normalized metric, guard-region response, and uncertainty interval. Report signed changes and raw units in addition to decibels. An improvement at a single receiver must not be described as protection across an unmeasured region.

J.6 Physical energy accounting and closure residual

For a control volume V, define EV as the stored energy included in the chosen inventory and define each boundary power consistently. One possible accounting form is dEV/dt = Pin − Pout − Pconversion, where Pconversion applies only to energy converted out of that defined inventory. When EV includes all internal thermal energy, conversion of mechanical or electromagnetic energy to heat inside V is an internal transfer and must not be subtracted as a loss. All terms have units of watts.

Define the measured closure residual ε_E as the sum of measured inputs minus measured outputs and storage changes under the chosen convention. Its uncertainty is determined from the covariance of measured channels and any bounded unmeasured terms. A residual inside the uncertainty budget is consistent with conservation but does not identify an underlying mechanism.

The original Christfield inward return-flow proposition may motivate an additional candidate transport term only after a carrier, physical flux units, coupling law, and measurement boundary have been defined. A term invented solely to close a residual has no independent predictive value.

J.7 Momentum and reaction-force ledger

For a mechanical or acoustic apparatus, measure or bound forces transferred through supports, mounts, and fixtures. The rate of momentum change in the selected volume equals the net applied force plus momentum flux through its boundary, with signs determined by the declared convention.

A measured decrease in local vibration may coincide with increased force on a support or a redistributed wave field. Both are ordinary physical explanations that must be tested before considering additional hypotheses.

No inference about resistance to impact, blast, or other hazardous loading may be drawn from the proposed benign disturbance measurements. Those regimes involve different constitutive laws, amplitudes, and safety risks.

J.8 Coherence and correlation as observables

Magnitude-squared coherence between measured channels x and y is γ²_xy(f) = |Sxy(f)|²/[Sxx(f) Syy(f)] for a specified spectral estimator and nonzero autospectra. It is a bounded statistical measure of frequency-specific linear association, not a direct measurement of an additional physical field.

A phase-order statistic can be defined as Cφ = |(1/N) Σj exp(iφj)|, which is dimensionless and lies between zero and one. Its value depends on which channels and time windows are included. It should be labelled a descriptive phase statistic rather than equated with the undefined Christfield coherence variable C.

If Christfield Dynamics proposes a distinct coherence field or gradient, that quantity requires its own operational definition, units, governing equation, and prospective observable. Existing spectral coherence measurements cannot supply those missing definitions by renaming them.

J.9 Model comparison and complexity penalties

Predefine a conventional model hierarchy: source-only response; passive apparatus and boundary effects; linear superposition; calibrated coupling; and justified nonlinear or adaptive terms. Compare predictions on held-out trials and include parameter uncertainty. A more flexible model may fit better without revealing a new physical mechanism.

An additional Christfield-specific model would need an explicit mathematical form, identifiable parameters, a dimensional coupling, and at least one quantitative prediction that differs from the strongest conventional baseline. The prediction must be frozen before confirmatory data are opened.

If both models predict the same observation within uncertainty, the experiment cannot discriminate between them. Report this as an interpretive limitation rather than declaring confirmation or falsification of the broader hypothesis.

J.11 Prediction gate and falsification criteria

Gate P0: identify a physical modality, source, apparatus, and registered primary endpoint. Gate P1: show calibrated instrumentation and a conventional model that makes held-out predictions. Gate P2: demonstrate reproducible active-versus-control effects with valid shams and source normalization.

Gate P3: demonstrate adequate energy and momentum accounting, including guard-region measurements and support reactions where relevant. Gate P4: specify a Christfield-specific quantitative alternative before collecting a new confirmatory dataset. Gate P5: seek independent replication and a prospective result that separates the models.

Failure at a gate can require revision or cessation of that experimental path. Passing P0 through P3 establishes an experimentally characterized physical control system, not necessarily an unconventional mechanism. No gate is represented as already passed for the protective application.

Appendix M. Prototype Integration, Control Implementation, and Verification

M.1 Integration philosophy and system boundary

The proposed non-barrier apparatus is a measurement-driven laboratory platform, not an operational protective field. Its first engineering function is to establish a reproducible relationship between controlled inputs and an independently measured, benign disturbance. The design must retain a clean boundary between conventional control performance and the separate hypothesis of inward return-flow organization.

Decompose the apparatus into six independently characterized actuation channels, one disturbance source, a reference source monitor, an independent receiver array, a deterministic timing subsystem, an experiment controller, a supervisory safety controller, and a raw-data archive. Define which interfaces carry energy, which carry measurements, and which carry control commands. No software-derived quantity should be called a physical energy flux without a calibrated conversion.

The Christfield Dynamics engineering release contributes a useful discipline of controlled geometry, model provenance, and feasibility gates. It does not supply a validated protective mechanism. The application-specific apparatus must independently demonstrate its transfer functions, safety envelope, and explanatory model.

M.2 Electrical and timing architecture

Each actuator channel shall have a separately measured drive voltage and current, a channel-enable state, a hardware output limit, and a fault state. Where the load is reactive, compute real power from synchronized voltage-current samples or a suitable calibrated power analyzer rather than multiplying RMS magnitudes without accounting for power factor.

Use one defined timing reference for the drive-generation and acquisition subsystems when technically feasible. Measure residual skew using an injected reference event, including the complete analog and digital signal path. The shared reference alone does not establish end-to-end phase alignment because amplifiers, transducers, filters, and propagation paths introduce frequency-dependent delay.

The design record shall contain a timing diagram for command issue, digital-to-analog conversion, actuator response, source-monitor sampling, independent receiver acquisition, interlock response, and data write. Record the latency distribution under maximum anticipated channel load. Do not infer closed-loop stability from nominal software scheduling rates.

M.3 Actuator drive chain and channel independence

For channel i, record command ui(t), measured terminal voltage vi(t), measured current ii(t), estimated electrical power pi(t), and an independently calibrated physical output where available. This establishes whether a commanded phase pattern corresponds to the physical phase pattern actually delivered by the array.

Test for channel-to-channel coupling through common supply impedance, shared ground returns, amplifier crosstalk, structural mounting, and field-mediated interactions. Characterize coupling with the other five channels connected in their normal inactive state. A single-channel bench measurement taken with all other hardware disconnected may not represent the assembled six-node platform.

If an amplifier saturates or a transducer response becomes nonlinear, flag the trial and either restrict the operating range or replace the linear transfer model. Saturation can produce harmonics and apparent changes in cancellation performance that should not be assigned to the proposed return-flow mechanism.

M.4 Control loop architecture and bounded operating modes

Provide four controlled operating modes: monitor-only, open-loop fixed pattern, closed-loop bounded control, and safe shutdown. Each mode requires explicit entry conditions, allowed command ranges, transition conditions, and logged exit causes. The monitor-only and open-loop modes establish the baseline needed to interpret any subsequent adaptive-control result.

The controller should operate on measured disturbances and independently verified sensor channels. Any adaptive phase or gain adjustment must have bounded update rates, anti-windup behavior where applicable, actuator saturation handling, and a defined fallback on sensor loss. Model the effect of delay on stability and require a conventional stability review before enabling feedback.

Keep a separate supervisory layer able to disable outputs without relying on the experiment controller. The supervisor shall monitor hardware limits and interlocks rather than infer safety from a favorable spatial-response index. A reduced receiver signal does not establish safe operation at the actuator, support, or guard region.

M.5 Six-node geometry and configuration records

Assign stable node identifiers R1 through R6. For each node record its surveyed center, orientation basis, mounting type, material stack, cable path, actuator model, calibration ID, and local coordinate transform. Identify the physical measurement datum and measurement uncertainty for each geometric parameter.

The geometry register shall explicitly separate a six-node laboratory test arrangement from any proposed full-body, room-scale, or building-scale application. A successful low-energy test at one scale does not justify scaling claims because wavelength, boundary conditions, source power, control authority, and energy pathways change.

When comparing geometries inspired by Christfield Dynamics or Φ-scaled lattices, register the exact geometry and conventional controls in advance. Compare physically equivalent source amplitudes, measurement regions, boundary materials, and sampling plans. A golden-ratio dimension is a design variable, not a privileged physical law until a discriminating prediction is established.

M.6 Sensor placement, independent observation, and spatial aliasing

The primary receiver array shall include points in the designated target region, the surrounding guard region, and reference locations capable of detecting source drift and boundary reflections. Survey the actual positions after assembly. Document sensor orientation and mounting effects because these may alter measured phase and amplitude.

Choose spatial sampling based on the highest frequency of interest and the expected field gradients. A grid that is too sparse can miss localized maxima and create a false impression of uniform attenuation. If a continuous field map is reconstructed from discrete sensors, report the interpolation method and its validation against withheld measurement positions.

Control sensors may be used to drive the apparatus, but the confirmatory endpoint should be measured by channels not used for optimization. This reduces the risk that the system learns to minimize a sensor-specific artifact rather than produce a reproducible spatial response.

M.7 Thermal and mechanical interfaces

Characterize heat generation in actuators, power electronics, cabling, and structural interfaces over the planned duty cycle. Measure temperatures at likely hot spots and document cooling assumptions. Changes in temperature can shift actuator impedance, sensor sensitivity, phase response, and mounting dimensions.

Mechanical mounting must be treated as part of the physical system. Supports can transmit vibration, store elastic energy, and alter boundary conditions. Include a support reaction or vibration measurement where the selected modality requires it, and document whether fixtures change between baseline and active conditions.

For any optical or electromagnetic laboratory extension, define separate exposure and electrical safety analyses. The present benign disturbance platform does not establish safety for optical power, RF fields, ionizing radiation, or high-energy mechanical events.

M.8 Hardware verification before experimental claims

Complete continuity, insulation, protective earth, interlock, current-limit, output-disable, and emergency-stop tests appropriate to the chosen apparatus. Verify that a commanded zero output produces an appropriately bounded physical output, including after a controller reboot, communication loss, or data-acquisition failure.

Verify the acquisition chain using a known injected signal. Record calibration residuals over the analysis bandwidth and verify the phase reference independently. Check for clipped samples, dropped frames, buffer overruns, and inconsistent timestamps before enabling any model-comparison analysis.

Only after apparatus verification may the laboratory proceed to registered baseline, individual-node, pairwise, and full-array trials. This sequence separates build qualification from scientific inference and prevents troubleshooting data from being counted as confirmatory evidence.

M.9 Firmware and software configuration control

Archive the exact firmware image, compiler settings, source revision, control parameters, data-acquisition configuration, and analysis version for each session. Use read-only experiment manifests where feasible. Record changes to controller gains, phase offsets, channel mapping, and filter coefficients as configuration changes rather than informal operator notes.

Software shall log every mode transition, interlock event, actuator enable, parameter update, and fault. Preserve monotonic sample counters alongside timestamps so that discontinuities can be identified even if wall-clock time is corrected during a session.

Analysis scripts should fail closed on missing calibration metadata or incompatible configuration IDs. A result derived from an unknown firmware state cannot be used as a clean confirmatory measurement.

M.10 Failure mode and effects review

Analyze loss of sensor signal, reversed channel mapping, timing drift, actuator runaway, thermal overload, unstable feedback, mechanical loosening, cable faults, amplifier clipping, source variation, and corrupted raw data. For each failure, specify the detectable symptom, severity in the chosen benign operating envelope, independent mitigation, and required operator response.

Do not treat a software watchdog as equivalent to an independent hardware shutdown. Where a single fault can lead to unacceptable output, the safety design must prevent that output even when the primary controller is malfunctioning.

Review the failure-mode register after each apparatus change and before a new test campaign. A successful earlier interlock test does not cover new power electronics, mounting conditions, or software pathways.

M.11 Engineering acceptance matrix and witness evidence

Each engineering requirement shall have an identifier, verification method, quantitative acceptance criterion, responsible reviewer, configuration applicability, recorded result, and disposition. Suitable methods include inspection, analysis, demonstration, and calibrated test. A requirement is not verified by a paragraph describing how it could be tested.

At minimum, the witness package should include the as-built survey, electrical schematic, wiring map, actuator transfer curves, receiver calibration, timing-skew measurement, interlock logs, source-monitor stability record, and one reproducible raw-data-to-result calculation.

The release decision should state one of three outcomes: approved for bounded feasibility testing, revision required, or deferred. It must not be phrased as approval of a protective field or as validation of the originating Christfield Dynamics hypothesis.

M.12 Assembly and commissioning sequence

Commission the platform in a fixed order: mechanical and electrical inspection; isolated instrumentation test; source-only baseline; inactive-hardware baseline; one-node actuation; six individual-node characterizations; fifteen pairwise checks; bounded open-loop six-node operation; and closed-loop operation only after a separate stability and safety review.

At each transition, compare the measured behavior against the prediction and the safety envelope. If a discrepancy exceeds the registered tolerance, pause progression, document the failure, and decide whether the conventional model, calibration, or hardware needs revision. Do not revise a model using confirmatory data while continuing to label that same dataset independent.

Commissioning records should identify the operator, witness, date, configuration, acceptance evidence, open anomalies, and explicit permission for the next stage. This makes the proposed research package suitable for review by an external engineering team.

Appendix N. Experimental Decision Architecture

N.1 Five independent scientific decision gates

Gate 1 asks whether the apparatus is safe and its measurements traceable. Gate 2 asks whether the spatial response is repeatable under blinded controls. Gate 3 asks whether a registered conventional forward model predicts the response. Gate 4 asks whether physical energy and momentum pathways are sufficiently observed to interpret the result. Gate 5 asks whether an alternative Christfield-specific model makes a quantitative prediction that succeeds on independent held-out data.

The gates are not interchangeable. A pass at Gate 2 does not imply a pass at Gate 3 or Gate 5. An unresolved model residual is not a new physical mechanism, and a model that fits only after parameter adjustment is not an independent prediction.

N.2 Model family and selection discipline

Register the disturbance-only baseline M0, the measured linear transfer model M1, and one or more higher-fidelity conventional models M2 as required by the carrier. The latter may include actuator coupling, nonlinear response, reflections, support dynamics, or temperature dependence when justified by calibration data.

Only after the conventional models and their uncertainty envelopes are frozen should a proposed alternative model be tested. The alternative must define its variables, units, coupling to observables, allowed parameters, and prediction that differs from the conventional model under a stated test condition.

The expression of an inward return pathway remains a conceptual research proposition until it is represented by a physically interpretable law. No additional energy term may be introduced solely to force the measured energy balance to close.

N.3 Statistical unit, replication, and missing data

Treat independent randomized trials or sessions as the unit of replication, not individual waveform samples. If the same apparatus, operator, and environment produce repeated measurements, account for correlation and session effects in uncertainty estimation.

Declare exclusions for saturation, invalid source monitoring, timing loss, safety interruption, and corrupted raw data before unblinding. Report the number of trials attempted, completed, excluded, and analyzed by condition. Retain all exclusions in an audit record.

A pilot may estimate variance and practical effect size, but a confirmatory claim requires a prospective sample-size rationale and a fixed decision threshold. Repeated exploratory optimization should be described as discovery work.

N.4 Adversarial alternative-explanation checklist

Before proposing an unconventional explanation, challenge the result against source amplitude drift, ambient changes, sensor orientation, channel crosstalk, signal processing leakage, timing offsets, amplifier saturation, mechanical coupling, passive scattering, spatial interference, feedback adaptation, and unmeasured energy redistribution.

For each alternative, record a specific test, expected direction of the artifact, actual observation, uncertainty, and whether the alternative remains plausible. A checklist item cannot be closed solely because an artifact seems unlikely.

Appendix O. Quantitative Engineering Design Basis and Calculation Examples

This appendix is a calculation-oriented engineering annex. The worked values are explicitly illustrative, not specifications, measured CHRISTOS® results, or validated predictions. The first-stage apparatus is restricted to a benign, low-energy disturbance selected and approved by a qualified laboratory. The optical Φ-Lattice engineering release is treated as a methodological reference, not as proof of a protective mechanism.

O.1 Design-basis hierarchy and frozen assumptions

The engineering design basis is organized into four levels. Level 0 is the originating hypothesis of structured inward return-flow organization toward a singularity-like center. Level 1 is the measurable physical system: source, propagation medium, sensors, actuators, structures, boundaries, power supplies, and controller. Level 2 is the conventional predictive model appropriate to the chosen carrier. Level 3 is a separately specified alternative model that must predict a measurable result not already captured by Level 2. A geometric motif is not by itself a constitutive law.

Freeze a single primary physical modality before designing the apparatus. Record density, stiffness or wave speed as applicable; temperature and environmental range; geometric survey tolerances; boundary impedance or reflectivity; source spectrum; sensor calibration; actuator bandwidth; timing reference; and safety limits. Each assumption requires a source, uncertainty, validation method, and owner. Unknown entries remain TBD and cannot be replaced with convenient nominal numbers.

The design-basis sheet shall have columns for identifier, quantity, symbol, SI unit, nominal value, plausible range, evidence source, validation method, and revision. If a parameter changes after the confirmatory protocol is frozen, the effect on predicted outcomes and test validity must be evaluated and recorded.

O.2 Six-node complex response model

For a linear time-invariant approximation at angular frequency ω, let dj(ω) denote the disturbance-only complex response at receiver j; let Hji(ω) denote the calibrated transfer from node input i to receiver j; and let ui(ω) denote that input. The predicted total response is yj(ω) = dj(ω) + Σ(i=1…6) Hji(ω)ui(ω) + ε_j(ω). All terms in the sum must share the same physical receiver units. The residual ε_j represents model error and measurement noise, not a presumed additional field.

The matrix H has dimensions m × 6 for m receiver channels at each frequency. If receiver measurements are pressure in pascals and node inputs are drive voltage in volts, Hji has units Pa/V. If a different carrier is chosen, both the response and transfer units must change accordingly. Mixing sensor voltage, physical pressure, and squared-amplitude indices without calibrated conversion is prohibited.

The rank and condition number of H matter. Highly correlated node columns can produce unstable inverse solutions and large command amplitudes even when a mathematical cancellation appears possible. Compute singular values and report the sensitivity of candidate controls to calibration perturbations. A solution that works only with unrealistic phase precision or excessive power is not a viable engineering operating point.

O.3 Constrained phase and amplitude selection

For a target receiver weighting matrix W and disturbance vector d, a conventional candidate objective is minimize J(u) = ||W(d + Hu)||² + λ||u||², subject to actuator, power, thermal, and safety constraints. The regularization parameter λ is a design choice selected using training or validation data, not tuned after confirmatory results are known.

The unconstrained least-squares expression u* = −(HᴴWᴴWH + λI)⁻¹HᴴWᴴWd is illustrative and requires the matrix to be invertible. The superscript ᴴ denotes complex conjugate transpose. The laboratory implementation must additionally enforce real-world command bounds, actuator phase lag, dynamic saturation, and time-domain safety limits.

If commands are restricted to equal amplitudes and adjustable phases, the feasible set is nonconvex. Document the optimization method, initialization, convergence checks, and sensitivity to local minima. Compare optimized commands with fixed-phase, randomized-phase, and sham conditions. Optimizer success is evidence of controllability under the fitted model, not evidence of unconventional physics.

O.4 Example cancellation geometry and limits

Consider a purely illustrative single receiver where the source response has normalized complex amplitude 1 + 0i. If a calibrated actuator contribution is −0.8 + 0i, the resulting amplitude is 0.2 and the squared-amplitude ratio relative to baseline is 0.04. The corresponding reduction is 10 log10(1/0.04) ≈ 14.0 dB. This arithmetic does not represent a measured device performance.

At the second receiver, the same illustrative actuator contribution is +0.4 + 0i. The total normalized amplitude becomes 1.4, with squared amplitude 1.96 and approximately +2.92 dB of response increase relative to the unity reference. A response increase at one point and decrease at another do not establish either net energy removal or net energy creation. Physical power accounting requires an explicit calibrated energy-flux or control-volume measurement.

For acoustic pressure, squared pressure is proportional to intensity only under additional assumptions about medium properties, propagation direction, and field conditions. Near-field and reverberant environments can violate simplistic conversions. Any joule-valued energy claim requires a physically defined flux and integration over a known surface and interval.

O.5 Phase timing and synchronization budget

For a pure positive time delay τ at frequency f, the signed phase shift is φ = −2πfτ under the exp(+iωt) convention. The magnitude is |φ| = 2πf|τ|. For timing standard uncertainty σt, the first-order phase uncertainty magnitude is σ_φ,t ≈ 2πfσt. This pure-delay model does not describe arbitrary dispersive electronics or mechanical phase lag, which must be characterized by measured frequency-dependent transfer functions.

Illustrative calculation: at f = 500 Hz, a relative timing error of 10 microseconds creates approximately 0.0314 radians, or 1.8 degrees, of phase error. At 2 kHz, the same delay creates about 7.2 degrees. These examples are sensitivity checks, not acceptance limits or selected operating frequencies.

Build a covariance-aware phase uncertainty budget covering acquisition-clock skew, driver latency, filter group delay, source reference error, sensor placement, actuator phase drift, and environmental propagation variability. Correlated errors shall not be added as though independent. The combined uncertainty must be tested against the phase sensitivity of the predicted effect.

O.6 Spatial sampling and held-out geometry

For a wave with speed c and frequency f, wavelength is λ_w = c/f when the assumed medium and propagation model justify that relation. A sample spacing substantially smaller than the shortest wavelength is typically needed to resolve spatial structure, but the exact grid must also reflect standing waves, near-field gradients, boundaries, and sensor footprint.

Illustrative acoustic calculation using c = 343 m/s and f = 1,000 Hz gives λ_w ≈ 0.343 m. A nominal 0.05 m grid interval corresponds to about 6.9 samples per wavelength along a surveyed line. This is not a universal sufficiency criterion; the laboratory must evaluate its geometry and field gradients.

Use a registered primary region and guard region. Predefine whether spatial aggregation weights equal area, volume, or a physically motivated quadrature rule. A grid with many closely spaced points does not create that many independent experimental replications. Confirmation should use withheld positions, a later session, or an independent apparatus.

O.7 Sensor noise, resolution, and effect detectability

Let Δ denote the preregistered active-minus-control endpoint and let σ_Δ denote its standard uncertainty. The detectable effect depends on noise, drift, calibration, source normalization, spatial correlation, and the number of independent trials. A single-channel digitizer resolution figure cannot substitute for an end-to-end uncertainty budget.

Illustrative calculation: if independent session-level differences have a sample standard deviation of 0.12 in a dimensionless normalized index, then for n = 16 independent sessions the estimated standard error of the sample mean is 0.12/√16 = 0.03. This is conditional on independence between sessions and does not include shared calibration bias, within-session clustering, or a power calculation. The sample standard deviation itself has uncertainty for n = 16.

Before data collection, determine the smallest effect of practical interest, select the inferential procedure, and simulate power under plausible within-session and between-session correlations. Include sensitivity analyses for missing sessions, source drift, and calibration uncertainty. A visually dramatic trace may still be inconclusive.

O.8 Real electrical power and thermal ledger

For a periodic electrical drive, real average power is the time average of instantaneous voltage multiplied by current, Preal = ⟨v(t)i(t)⟩. For a sinusoidal steady-state signal this may be written Vrms Irms cos θ, where θ is the voltage-current phase angle. Multiplying rms voltage and rms current without the power factor gives apparent power, not necessarily real power.

Illustrative calculation: for a sinusoidal steady-state channel with rms voltage Vrms, rms current Irms, and displacement power factor cos θ, the real power is Preal = Vrms Irms cos θ, and the electrical energy entering the channel over an interval T at constant average power is E = Preal T. Specific numerical values are not disclosed in this public version. The result does not establish how much energy reaches a target disturbance field; losses and changes in storage must be measured separately. For nonsinusoidal signals, use the sampled average of v(t)i(t) rather than this simplified power-factor expression.

Instrument all relevant channels and the supply input where feasible. Record standby consumption, driver loss, actuator heating, support dissipation, and measured disturbance flux. For time-varying operation, integrate measured v(t)i(t) over the registered interval. Any claimed conversion pathway must be independently measurable rather than inferred from a reduced receiver reading.

O.9 Momentum and mechanical reaction closure

Where the chosen modality can transfer momentum, identify the apparatus supports, reaction forces, and boundary flux. The integral momentum balance must include time variation of momentum inside the control volume, external applied forces, and net momentum flux through its boundary. A device cannot be assigned reaction-free operation because a local receiver records a reduced amplitude.

For mechanical or acoustic testing, support loads and mount vibrations may introduce alternative coupling paths. Use witness sensors or force measurements where appropriate. A model that includes the propagation field but omits structural supports can yield misleading residuals.

If the laboratory cannot measure the dominant momentum pathways, label the momentum ledger incomplete. A residual is an unresolved metrology result until conventional pathways and their uncertainties have been quantified.

Define startup, calibration, bounded operation, degraded operation, and safe shutdown as separate states.

This appendix converts the proposed non-barrier system from an architectural narrative into a traceable, testable set of engineering interfaces. Q.1 The three-layer technical architecture

The study shall maintain three explicitly separate layers. Layer A is a conventional physical plant: a disturbance source, a bounded propagation medium, sensors, actuators, structures, and power supplies. Layer B is an estimation and control architecture that can change the measured field through known interactions, including interference, feedback, impedance, damping, and boundary modification. Layer C is the proposed Christfield-specific return-flow mechanism, which is not introduced into calculations as a physical energy sink until its variables, units, governing law, and distinctive predictions have been defined.

Each system diagram shall use solid lines for measurable signals and physical energy paths, dashed lines for calculated or inferred states, and a separately labelled hypothesis path for any unverified Christfield-specific coupling. A schematic that draws a return arrow without identifying a carrier and measurable boundary is a conceptual diagram, not a conservation proof.

This layering allows conventional engineering progress even if Layer C remains unresolved. The apparatus can characterize phase-dependent spatial organization without asserting a novel force, while the hypothesis remains available for prospective testing if it yields a quantitatively different outcome.

Q.2 Complex-valued multi-input, multi-output response model

For an approximately linear time-invariant plant at angular frequency ω, write y(ω) = d(ω) + H(ω)u(ω) + n(ω), where y is an m-element measured response vector, d is the disturbance-only response at those receivers, H is an m-by-6 transfer matrix, u is the six-element actuator input vector, and n represents measurement noise. Each term must use compatible physical units. H has units of receiver response per actuator command unit, not necessarily dimensionless.

The matrix shall be estimated from independent actuator characterization, with amplitude and phase uncertainty for every complex entry. When interactions change the plant, a single H is inadequate; a condition-dependent model or nonlinear state-space model must be registered. The experiment must not treat H as exact merely because it is represented by a table of complex numbers.

In a practical design, reserve receivers that are not used to optimize u. A response minimum at the optimization microphone or vibration sensor is insufficient evidence of spatial control; the prediction must hold at withheld positions.

Q.3 Regularized control solution and physical constraints

For a specified weighting matrix W and positive regularization λ, a conventional candidate command is u* = −(HᴴWH + λI)⁻¹HᴴWd. The superscript H denotes conjugate transpose. This expression assumes a linear model and an unconstrained complex command; it is a design calculation, not a physically guaranteed optimum.

Before implementation, constrain actuator voltage, current, thermal load, slew rate, frequency, and phase latency. A constrained optimizer may minimize ||W½(d + Hu)||² + λ||u||² subject to independently enforced hardware limits. State whether input limits are on commanded amplitude or measured real electrical power; these are not interchangeable.

For rank-deficient H, the regularizer controls numerical sensitivity but cannot create physical controllability. Report singular values, condition number, actuator authority, and the sensitivity of the solution to calibration errors. A visually impressive predicted null may be unreliable if it requires commands along nearly unobservable directions.

Q.4 Worked illustrative two-receiver cancellation example

Consider a dimensionless normalized example with disturbance d = [1, 1]ᵀ and a single controlled contribution h = [1, −0.5]ᵀ. Choosing actuator command u = −1 gives y = d + hu = [0, 1.5]ᵀ. The first receiver falls from amplitude 1 to 0 while the second rises from 1 to 1.5. The sum of squared normalized amplitudes changes from 2 to 2.25, illustrating that local cancellation need not represent globally reduced response.

These normalized squared amplitudes are not joules or watts. Converting them into energy or power requires a medium-specific constitutive relationship, sensor calibration, spatial integration, and an appropriate time interval. A physically complete analysis must also include actuator power and relevant boundary fluxes.

The example demonstrates why the manuscript requires a target region and a guard region. A non-barrier claim cannot be based solely on the smallest observed value at a chosen receiver.

Q.5 Model identification with independent validation

Calibration data are used to estimate H and its uncertainty. Model-selection data may determine whether a linear transfer model is adequate or whether nonlinear coupling, support motion, or boundary corrections must be included. A third, untouched confirmatory dataset tests predictions made with frozen parameters. The three roles shall be documented before any confirmatory run.

An appropriate diagnostic is the normalized residual rj = (ymeasured,j − ypredicted,j)/σj where σj represents the combined uncertainty at receiver j. For correlated receivers, a covariance-weighted residual statistic is more appropriate than treating all rj as independent. The covariance matrix must be estimated with adequate replication and regularized only under a documented rule.

Large residuals are a prompt to check calibration, saturation, timing, source drift, and unmodelled conventional physics. They are not evidence of the hypothesized return-flow pathway unless a separate constrained model prospectively predicts their form.

Q.6 Phase synchronization and spatial metrology

For a pure time delay τ, phase shift at frequency f is 2πfτ. The phase uncertainty attributable to timing uncertainty σt is approximately σ_φ = 2πfσt, valid for the specified delay approximation. At 1 kHz, 10 microseconds of relative timing uncertainty produces approximately 0.0628 rad, or 3.6 degrees, of phase uncertainty. This is a numerical example, not an adopted design tolerance.

Timing uncertainty includes trigger skew, sampling-clock stability, analog filter group delay, driver latency, transducer dynamics, and analysis-window alignment. A common clock helps but does not remove unequal analog delays. Calibrate the full signal chain using a physical reference rather than assuming identical electronic channels.

Positioning uncertainty also matters. If a propagating wave has wavelength ℓ, a path-length error δx produces an approximate phase error of 2πδx/ℓ under a simple plane-wave approximation. Boundary reflections and near-field geometry can invalidate that approximation and require a spatial forward model.

Compute singular values across the intended band, identify poorly controlled modes, and record the uncertainty in each estimate.

Place primary sensors to measure the intended response and independent witness sensors to detect redistribution, source drift, or mounting changes. A sensor placed at a predicted null can be excellent for optimization but poor for determining whether the larger region improved. The spatial endpoint must be defined before tuning the array.

For a dynamic controller, use a state-space representation ẋ = Ax + Bu + Ed and y = Cx + Du + n only after identifying state meanings and units. An estimator cannot infer unobservable physical states solely by adding variables to its software model.

Appendix S. System Identification, Uncertainty Propagation, and Acceptance Logic

All calculations and procedures are prospective; no protective capability or nonconventional energy pathway has been measured. This appendix strengthens the bridge between the existing six-node design, conventional predictions, and any future Christfield-specific test.

S.1 Measurement model and notation

For N instrumented actuators and M receiver channels at angular frequency ω, define the complex receiver vector y(ω) ∈ ℂ^M, disturbance vector d(ω) ∈ ℂ^M, actuator command vector u(ω) ∈ ℂ^N, and transfer matrix H(ω) ∈ ℂ^(M×N). In the local linear regime, y = d + Hu + ε, where ε includes stochastic noise and unmodelled response. The dimensions of each column of H are receiver-unit per command-unit; the command must be physically specified.

For the proposed six-node array N = 6, but the receiver count M need not equal six. A six-by-six model is appropriate only if exactly six receiver channels are selected. Never infer six receiver dimensions from six actuator nodes. When sources or nodes couple, H may depend on operating point and simultaneous loading; a fixed linear model then requires a verified validity envelope.

The observable y may be pressure in pascals, acceleration in metres per second squared, displacement in metres, or another calibrated quantity. A generic coherence index is not a replacement for a calibrated field observable. Define Fourier normalization, reference phase, sample window, and sign convention before combining complex measurements.

S.2 Experimental identification of H

Estimate one transfer column at a time by applying a known bounded input to node i and subtracting the synchronized disturbance-only reference. The complex ratio Hji = Δyj/ui is meaningful only when ui is nonzero, the disturbance is sufficiently stationary or independently monitored, and the response remains within the locally linear range.

Use repeated excitations and a frequency grid dense enough to capture resonances. Record the actual drive signal at the actuator interface rather than relying solely on a software command. Characterize the measurement chain with actuator disabled to estimate electrical pickup, cable crosstalk, and sensor susceptibility.

Cross-check each estimated column with a second excitation amplitude and a different excitation sequence. An amplitude-dependent H or phase shift indicates a nonlinear or time-varying plant. Either narrow the operating envelope or introduce a validated alternative model; do not average incompatible transfer functions into a falsely precise result.

S.3 Frequency-domain estimation and coherence

For a stationary, approximately linear single-input channel, a spectral transfer estimate may be formed as H1(f) = Syu(f)/Suu(f), using a documented cross-spectrum convention. Report estimator type, averaging count, window, overlap, and input spectral support. The estimated transfer may be biased by output noise, input noise, or feedback correlations; the estimator choice must match the measurement conditions.

Magnitude-squared coherence γ²(f) = |Syu(f)|²/[Suu(f)Syy(f)] ranges from zero to one under standard assumptions. High coherence supports a repeatable linear association at a frequency, not an unconventional mechanism. Low coherence may reflect low signal-to-noise ratio, nonlinear response, multiple unmeasured inputs, or nonstationarity.

When feedback is active, ordinary open-loop spectral estimators can be biased because commands respond to measured disturbances. Characterize the plant in a safe open-loop identification phase or use a justified closed-loop identification method with independent excitation.

S.4 Spatial response and nonlocal redistribution audit

Define a target region Ω_T and guard region Ω_G before collecting confirmatory data. A registered response index may be JT = Σj wj |yj|² over target sensors, with nonnegative quadrature weights wj. The units are receiver-unit squared times the units of wj; JT is not automatically physical energy or power.

For each active configuration compute ΔJT = JT,active − JT,control and ΔJG analogously. Report both signed changes, spatial maps, and source-normalized quantities. An improvement at the target accompanied by deterioration in the guard region is compatible with ordinary field redistribution and should be reported as such.

Finite spatial sampling can miss lobes and nodes. Require a model-based spatial sampling analysis, survey uncertainty, and at least one held-out map. A local sensor minimum cannot establish whole-body, room-wide, or dome-wide protection.

S.5 Weighted optimization and constrained actuation

An illustrative conventional controller may minimize J(u) = (d + Hu)ᴴ W(d + Hu) + λuᴴRu, where W and R are positive semidefinite weights and λ ≥ 0. Under a positive-definite normal matrix, the unconstrained solution is u* = −(HᴴWH + λR)⁻¹HᴴWd. The expression is a baseline optimization method, not a Christfield law.

Physical deployment requires constraints on voltage, current, temperature, displacement, acoustic output, slew rate, and emergency stop. The unconstrained optimum can violate these limits. Solve a constrained problem or apply an independently verified saturation strategy and then recompute the predicted field using the actual bounded command.

Optimize against the target and guard regions jointly. If the controller only minimizes a target sensor, it may exploit a measurement blind spot. Add robustness penalties for uncertainty in H, d, node position, and timing. Compare against simple fixed-phase and equal-power baselines.

S.6 Robustness to model uncertainty

Let H = H0 + ΔH, where H0 is the estimated transfer matrix and ΔH captures bounded model error. A robust design must report the uncertainty set and show how predicted improvement changes over plausible perturbations. The magnitude of ΔH should be inferred from independent validation and environmental sensitivity, not selected to make the model appear reliable.

Perturb actuator gain, phase, node placement, support stiffness, and sensor calibration one at a time, then jointly where correlations are plausible. Report worst-case and typical-case response. A pattern that only works for a single precisely fitted H0 may be unsuitable for experimental confirmation.

Validate on independent sessions and configurations. If residuals increase systematically after a temperature change or node remounting, the result should trigger model revision rather than a claim of a new physical interaction.

S.7 Phase and timing uncertainty propagation

Under the declared exp(+iωt) convention, a pure positive delay τ produces Δφ(f) = −2πfτ. Its timing standard uncertainty contributes σφ ≈ 2πf στ radians in magnitude. This applies only to pure-delay uncertainty; actuator dispersion, propagation uncertainty, and nonlinear phase response require separate terms. With exp(−iωt), the signed delay phase reverses.

For a differentiable output metric g(z) with uncertain parameter vector z and covariance matrix Σz, the first-order variance approximation is Var(g) ≈ (∇g)ᵀΣz(∇g). Retain covariance terms for shared clocks, common calibration references, and spatial survey errors. Where linearization is inadequate, use a documented Monte Carlo propagation with convergence checks.

Illustrative only: at 500 Hz, a 10-microsecond relative timing uncertainty corresponds to about 0.0314 radians or 1.8 degrees. This is not a specified timing tolerance. The allowable uncertainty must be derived from the smallest phase-dependent effect the pilot intends to resolve.

S.8 Feedback architecture and stability margin

Separate the safety supervisor from the research controller. The safety supervisor enforces independent operating bounds and remains effective if the research computer freezes, produces invalid output, or loses sensor communication. Record the physical fail-safe state and verify it under simulated faults.

For a conventional negative-feedback loop with plant P(s) and controller K(s), the sensitivity is S(s) = [I + P(s)K(s)]⁻¹ under the stated sign convention and compatible dimensions. Stability cannot be inferred from a single low-error trace. Examine gain and phase margins or an appropriate multivariable stability criterion, actuator saturation, delay, and uncertainty.

Begin with passive monitoring and bounded open-loop characterization. Permit closed-loop operation only after documented stability review, independent shutdown verification, and an approved operating envelope. Treat adaptive or phase-seeking behavior as a distinct software configuration requiring renewed review.

Electrical power in a single channel is the time average of instantaneous voltage-current product p(t) = v(t)i(t); RMS voltage multiplied by RMS current is apparent power unless power factor is included. Measure real input power during simultaneous operation, including driver and control electronics when reporting system consumption.

For the chosen physical carrier, define a control volume V and a complete energy inventory. With outward energy flux FE and separately accounted source power Pin, a total-energy balance is dEV/dt + ∮∂V FE·n dA = Pin, provided the inventory includes internal heat and all other stored forms and no additional boundary source is omitted. A reduced carrier-specific inventory may instead include a separately measured conversion term; the same heat cannot be counted twice.

Provide an energy closure residual and combined uncertainty, including unmeasured boundaries. A local decrease in squared receiver amplitude cannot be labelled removed energy. A proposed return pathway remains a hypothesis until it has a physical flux law, SI dimensions, an observable receiving boundary, and independent measurements.

A qualitative description of return flow or a post hoc coherence metric is insufficient.

The proposed relationship X = δC/δΨ remains operationally unresolved unless C, Ψ, the derivative type, and physical dimensions are specified. The protection study shall not silently substitute a sensor index for C or treat the expression as an established energy-transfer law. Register a separate falsifiable prediction before examining held-out results.

S.12 Model comparison and statistical decision rules

Predeclare the primary endpoint, effect direction, minimum scientifically meaningful effect, number of independent sessions, treatment of multiple comparisons, and uncertainty method. An exploratory optimum selected from many phase patterns requires a fresh confirmatory dataset to avoid selection bias.

Compare M0 (disturbance and passive apparatus), M1 (calibrated linear response), M2 (validated conventional nonlinear or boundary-aware model if justified), and an additional MCF only when its equations and predictions are specified in advance. Report held-out predictive errors and uncertainty rather than relying only on goodness of fit to training data.

Possible outcomes include no reproducible effect; reproducible effect explained by conventional physics; reproducible unexplained residual requiring further investigation; or a prespecified additional model that improves held-out predictions. None of these outcomes alone establishes safe protection from hazardous exposure.

S.13 Engineering acceptance test hierarchy

Acceptance gate A: documentation integrity. Verify controlled drawings, electrical interface definitions, firmware revision, calibration certificates, and a complete hazard review. Gate B: instrument integrity. Demonstrate independent source monitoring, sensor linearity, timing synchronization, and traceable raw data.

Gate C: bounded hardware behavior. Verify actuator output, real power, thermal behavior, passive and sham states, and independent shutdown. Gate D: model integrity. Confirm that individually identified transfer functions predict withheld pairwise and full-array response within the registered uncertainty.

Gate E: experimental integrity. Complete randomized controls, held-out spatial maps, preregistered analysis, and adverse-event reporting. Gate F: interpretive integrity. Separate observed field changes from energy closure and from Christfield-specific explanatory claims. A failed gate prevents advancement without a documented corrective action.

S.14 Reproducible computational workflow

Archive the exact source code or executable, solver version, mesh or spatial discretization, material parameters, boundary conditions, input waveforms, random seeds, and numerical tolerances. Provide a machine-readable configuration file linking each simulation to a controlled apparatus identifier.

For finite-element or wave models, demonstrate convergence of the registered receiver outputs, not merely the global residual norm. Repeat a subset with a different mesh strategy or independent numerical implementation. Numerical agreement between two scripts sharing the same erroneous boundary condition is not independent verification.

Produce a traceability table connecting every plotted prediction to its input dataset, model version, calibration record, and analysis command. Disclose missing source code or unavailable proprietary components as reproducibility limitations.

S.15 Example preregistration statement

The laboratory will test whether the registered six-node configuration changes a prespecified source-normalized response index within a benign target region relative to sham operation. The primary analysis will use independent sessions as the replication unit, report the guard-region response, and compare measured changes with frozen conventional-model predictions.

Phase patterns, geometry, frequency band, spatial grid, allowable exclusions, sample size, and decision thresholds will be specified in a signed protocol before confirmatory acquisition. No parameter will be retuned using the held-out dataset. The experiment will not test impact resistance, radiation shielding, or human safety.

If a repeatable residual exceeds the registered uncertainty envelope, the result will be described as unresolved pending independent conventional-model challenges. The result will not be labelled a demonstrated inward return-flow mechanism without a prospective additional model and independent replication.

Appendix U. Prototype 1B Engineering Design Definition and Drawing Release

This appendix develops a controlled mechanical, electrical, and instrumentation handoff for the proposed six-node benign laboratory test. It does not specify an unbounded protective apparatus. All dimensions, ratings, and limits must be selected by the qualified laboratory following modality selection and hazard review. The engineering release of the optical Φ-Lattice remains a separate feasibility package, not approval of this apparatus.

U.1 Configuration hierarchy and release boundaries

Define four configuration levels: the experimental question and endpoint (L0); physical carrier and site envelope (L1); actuator, sensor, structure, and controller configuration (L2); and individual serialized build records (L3). A change at L1 requires review of all downstream calculations. Changes at L2 require rechecking transfer functions, timing, and interlocks. A change at L3 requires an as-built deviation record even when the drawing revision is unchanged.

Each configuration baseline shall contain a drawing index, bill of materials, firmware hash, model version, calibration certificates, wiring schedule, and safety review. A drawing marked preliminary is not an instruction to manufacture. A release signature certifies documentation completeness for a specified test, not physical validity of the Christfield hypothesis.

The six-node test is an implementation choice for investigating spatially distributed control. Neither six nodes nor toroidal geometry is asserted to be a necessary property of the originating inward return-flow proposition.

U.2 Drawing package and required views

The assembly drawing shall show an orthographic top view, two elevations, a dimensioned node-coordinate table, reference datums, source and sensor positions, structural restraints, and the defined measurement volume. Include cable routes and keep-outs to avoid changing the physical boundary conditions during repositioning.

Each node subassembly drawing shall identify its mechanical attachment, adjustment degrees of freedom, cable strain relief, orientation mark, electrical connector, identification plate, and inspection dimensions. Specify a tolerance only after sensitivity analysis shows its effect on phase and spatial response. Arbitrarily tight tolerances raise cost without strengthening inference.

The instrumentation drawing shall separately depict controller telemetry and independent measurement channels. The safety drawing shall show the hardwired disable path, source shutdown, and monitoring points. The drawings must share a common configuration identifier so that measurement data can be traced to a single as-built state.

U.3 Geometric survey and tolerance allocation

For frequency f and wave speed c in the test medium, wavelength is λ = c/f. For a locally planar harmonic wave represented as exp[i(ωt − k·r)] under the declared exp(+iωt) temporal convention, a positive displacement Δr along propagation changes the signed phase by Δφ = −2πΔr/λ. Its magnitude is |Δφ| = 2π|Δr|/λ. The opposite spatial phase convention reverses the sign. For nonplanar or dispersive fields, use the measured local wavevector and propagation model; this relationship is not a universal placement tolerance.

As an illustrative acoustic example only, c = 343 m/s and f = 1000 Hz give λ = 0.343 m. A 1 mm displacement corresponds to approximately 0.0183 rad, or 1.05 degrees, along the propagation direction. The example does not specify the planned test frequency or required machining tolerance.

The laboratory shall construct a tolerance stack that includes node position, orientation, sensor mounting, source position, and thermal drift. Where errors share a datum or support, treat their correlations explicitly. The stack must be tested against the maximum acceptable prediction uncertainty rather than against an aesthetic geometry target.

U.4 Mechanical support, mounting, and modal interactions

The mounting structure is part of the experiment. In vibration tests, its compliance and resonances can alter transfer functions; in acoustic tests, it can scatter waves and introduce reflections. Characterize the structure in its inactive state and after adding cables and transducers. An empty-room baseline is not a substitute for an installed-hardware baseline.

Use mechanically repeatable mounts with documented preload or fastening procedure. Record the condition of elastomeric interfaces, clamps, and support contacts. If a node is repositioned, the experiment must identify whether the previous model remains applicable. Do not infer physical field organization from a response that tracks mount resonance.

The independent reviewer should receive measured or bounded support properties and a list of mechanical assumptions in the forward model. If the structure cannot be adequately characterized, reduce the experimental claim to a more limited observable.

U.5 Electrical power and connector schedule

Each actuator channel shall have an individual connector identifier, pinout, voltage and current rating, return conductor, protective device, and test point. Separate power wiring from sensitive analog measurement leads where feasible. Record cable length, shielding termination, and grounding arrangement because these can alter noise and phase.

Power accounting requires simultaneous voltage and current acquisition at the appropriate boundary: Preal = mean[v(t)i(t)] over a registered interval. The product of nominal voltage and nominal current is not a measured real power value, especially for reactive loads. Log per-channel power and the controller and driver overhead separately.

For each supply and driver, document behavior under disconnected load, shorted output, overtemperature, and loss of control communication. Hardware protective functions must not depend exclusively on the same processor being tested for feedback performance.

U.6 Timing architecture and synchronization test

Define one master timing reference and specify which channels are synchronously sampled and which use timestamps or reconstructed alignment. A common clock reduces relative drift but does not eliminate analog filter delay, actuator phase lag, trigger skew, or time-of-flight variation.

Perform a loopback timing test that routes a known reference signal through the acquisition and drive paths. Measure offset and jitter over the duration of a full experimental session, not just at startup. Retest after firmware changes, channel swaps, and relevant temperature changes.

The timing acceptance condition shall be derived from the phase budget at the highest analyzed frequency. For pure delay, σφ ≈ 2πfσt; correlated errors and model uncertainty must be added through an appropriate covariance calculation. A measured phase shift is not evidence of a novel physical mechanism unless conventional latency and propagation are excluded.

U.7 Sensor layout and calibration transfer

The sensor layout shall include primary endpoints, guard-region coverage, source reference, and at least one position reserved for independent verification. Record each sensor’s orientation, coupling method, local boundary condition, and calibrated transfer function. A sensor moved to a new mount may require recalibration of the measurement chain.

Before each session, perform zero or noise checks, reference-amplitude checks, phase-alignment checks, and clipping checks. Preserve the raw measurements and calibration factors. Record uncertainty contributions from instrument gain, frequency response, position, environmental variation, and source normalization.

A receiver signal should be converted to an energy or power quantity only when the relevant physical constitutive relation, impedance or flux calibration, and measurement geometry are known. Squared voltage or pressure is otherwise a response index with specified units.

U.8 Controller states and hardware interlock truth table

The minimum control states are SAFE_OFF, SELF_TEST, BASELINE, SHAM, OPEN_LOOP_BOUNDED, CLOSED_LOOP_BOUNDED, FAULT_LATCHED, and MAINTENANCE. Each state has permitted outputs, required monitors, entry conditions, exit conditions, and an immutable event log. Startup and communication loss must default to SAFE_OFF or FAULT_LATCHED.

Independent hardware protection shall remove actuator energy when a critical current, temperature, motion, or enclosure condition exceeds the approved limit. The software shall also implement bounded commands, rate limits, and a watchdog, but these are secondary safeguards. No human-exposure mode is included.

Commission the state transitions with simulated faults before connecting active actuators. Verify that emergency shutdown works during controller failure, data-logging failure, and timing-reference loss. Record observed shutdown latency and compare it with the laboratory hazard assessment.

U.9 Firmware and data-flow design

Separate the real-time control task, supervisory safety reporting, and raw measurement recorder. The controller may calculate a response estimate but must not overwrite independent receiver data. Use monotonically increasing sample or event counters so dropped packets can be detected.

Firmware release records shall include source revision, compiled binary hash, build options, parameter file, sample rate, controller gains, actuator bounds, watchdog settings, and a list of known limitations. Any parameter changed during a confirmatory trial shall trigger a protocol deviation entry.

The analysis interface shall export raw waveforms and metadata without imposing an undisclosed smoothing or phase correction. All transformations from raw data to the primary endpoint must be documented and reproducible.

U.10 Verification from component to integrated assembly

Component verification covers identification, electrical continuity, safe output bounds, sensor calibration, and independent interlock operation. Subsystem verification covers a single node, sensor channel, clock distribution, and source monitor. Integration verification covers coupling, simultaneous power, pairwise response, and full-array control.

Acceptance evidence shall link each requirement to a test ID, procedure revision, calibration reference, recorded result, uncertainty, pass/fail criterion, and approving reviewer. A pass criterion cannot be invented after the results are known. Any unresolved requirement must remain open with a clear effect on test readiness.

No claim of protective performance may be derived from successful assembly, firmware operation, or closed-loop suppression at a selected benign receiver. These outcomes qualify apparatus behavior only.

U.11 As-built deviation and nonconformance handling

Every discrepancy between the drawing package and the assembled device receives a nonconformance identifier, description, potential influence on the endpoint, proposed disposition, and approval record. Appendix V. Traceable Analysis Example and Model-Comparison Worked Case

The following is a transparent synthetic calculation to illustrate a reproducible analysis workflow. The numerical values are invented solely for demonstration. They are not measurements of the CHRISTOS® apparatus, a prediction of Christfield Dynamics, or evidence of a protective effect.

V.1 Two-receiver complex-amplitude example

Let the disturbance-only complex amplitudes at two receivers be d = [1.00 + 0.00i, 0.60 + 0.00i]. Let the controlled contribution from the array be H u = [−0.70 + 0.00i, +0.50 + 0.00i]. The total response is y = d + H u = [0.30 + 0.00i, 1.10 + 0.00i]. The quantities are dimensionless normalized amplitudes in this example.

At receiver 1 the squared-amplitude index falls from 1.00 to 0.09, a 91% reduction. At receiver 2 it rises from 0.36 to 1.21, an increase of approximately 236%. Across these two points the squared-amplitude sum changes from 1.36 to 0.09 + 1.21 = 1.30, a decrease of 0.06. This two-point sum is neither a complete spatial integral nor a conserved energy ledger.

The numerical case demonstrates why reporting only the first receiver would conceal a substantial redistribution. It also shows that an incomplete spatial sample cannot determine where energy went, or whether total physical energy changed.

V.2 Error correction and arithmetic audit

The two-point baseline index is 1.00² + 0.60² = 1.36. The active index is 0.30² + 1.10² = 1.30. The relative change is (1.30 − 1.36)/1.36 = −0.0441, or approximately −4.41%. This is an index computed at two points, not a physical flux integral.

For receiver 1, 10 log₁₀(1.00/0.09) ≈ +10.46 dB of normalized squared-amplitude response reduction. For receiver 2, 10 log₁₀(0.36/1.21) ≈ −5.26 dB, a response increase. These are dimensionless illustrative response ratios, not measured acoustic or electromagnetic power flows. They are reportable as exact ratios only when both compared quantities are reliably quantified above the applicable measurement floor.

A reviewer should independently recompute every value from the listed amplitudes. If the model uses complex transfer functions with nonzero imaginary components, calculate magnitudes using |z|² = Re(z)² + Im(z)² rather than squaring the real component alone.

V.3 Prediction error and withheld validation

Suppose a conventional model predicts a held-out complex amplitude of 0.32 + 0.04i at the primary receiver while the measured value is 0.30 + 0.02i. The complex residual is −0.02 − 0.02i and its magnitude is approximately 0.0283 in the same amplitude units. This is a synthetic illustration of a residual, not an experimental observation.

Whether the residual is meaningful depends on the full measurement covariance, model parameter uncertainty, and prespecified test statistic. A residual that is larger than digitizer noise can still be explained by unmodelled source drift, geometry error, boundary effects, or actuator nonlinearity.

Any additional Christfield-specific model must state its extra parameters and unique prediction before the held-out data are acquired. Merely adding an adjustable residual term to fit the observed difference does not constitute independent validation.

V.4 Synthetic trial register and analysis sequence

The reproducible workflow begins with a frozen configuration record and randomization seed, then acquires baseline and sham records, individual-node calibrations, pairwise tests, and confirmatory full-array trials. Raw data, sensor calibration, and model predictions are archived separately before the blind condition labels are opened.

Calculate the registered endpoint for every independent trial, preserve failures and exclusions, then estimate effect size and uncertainty at the trial or session level. Do not treat thousands of samples from one run as thousands of independent experiments. Report the number of sessions and configurations alongside the sample count.

Publish the model residuals, guard-region responses, power measurements, and negative controls with the primary result. A laboratory should be able to reproduce the table from raw records without access to unpublished researcher judgment.

V.5 Decision table for scientific interpretation

Case A: the active configuration yields no reproducible effect beyond sham. Disposition: the tested configuration does not support the proposed effect under the registered conditions; revise or stop rather than claim hidden success.

Case B: a reproducible response is predicted by a calibrated conventional model. Disposition: valid engineering result for controlled interference or feedback; no additional physical mechanism established.

Case C: a reproducible residual remains after qualified conventional-model, calibration, and uncertainty challenges. Disposition: unresolved anomaly requiring new preregistered tests and independent replication; no immediate inference of a protective field.

Case D: a separately specified additional model predicts a novel held-out effect and independent replication confirms it. Disposition: evidence supporting that model within its tested domain, with broader claims reserved for further experiments.

Appendix X. Physical-Carrier Selection and Transferability Limits

This appendix introduces a carrier-specific decision framework. The proposed return-flow architecture is preserved as a hypothesis, but a protective application cannot be inferred from optical feasibility, benign interference, or feedback alone. All examples in this appendix are design analyses, not experimental observations.

X.1 Carrier-specific governing laws

Acoustic pressure waves, elastic vibration, electromagnetic fields, and thermal transport obey different constitutive relationships and boundary conditions. A phase-controlled pressure reduction cannot be interpreted as attenuation of an impact or electromagnetic hazard. For each proposed application, specify the transported quantity, conservation law, field variables, and energy-flux expression before selecting hardware.

The minimum modelling dossier identifies the state vector, material properties, source terms, domain boundaries, initial conditions, nonlinear regimes, and measurable output. An experimentally fitted transfer matrix may predict a limited operating region without proving a universal physical mechanism. State the range in which each approximation is valid.

Any additional Christfield-specific term must enter the governing equations with a defined physical dimension, sign convention, and coupling rule. An undefined coherence variable is not a substitute for a source term, and a numerical residual is not a measured return channel.

X.2 Acoustic feasibility carrier

For a linear small-signal acoustic study in a homogeneous medium, pressure p and particle velocity v can be modelled by the linearized continuity and momentum equations. The resulting wave equation is a baseline, not a proposed Christfield law. A laboratory must account for reflections, room modes, temperature dependence, transducer directivity, and microphone phase response.

The instantaneous acoustic intensity in a simple plane-wave regime is p(t)v(t), with time-average intensity obtained from the corresponding product. Pressure-squared alone is not generally a complete energy-flux measurement in a standing-wave field. Where intensity cannot be measured, explicitly label the endpoint a pressure-response index.

An acoustic feasibility experiment can investigate reproducible phase-dependent spatial organization at low energy. It cannot certify protection from high-energy pressure impulses or generalize beyond the tested band and geometry.

X.3 Elastic and structural vibration carrier

For a vibration study, define the mechanical plant through mass, damping, stiffness, and applied forces. In a linear lumped approximation, Mq¨ + Dq˙ + Kq = Bu + Fd. The state q has units of displacement; M, D, and K carry their corresponding mechanical units. Modelled structural modes, support reactions, and sensor mass loading must be included.

An apparent reduction at one accelerometer can arise from a shifted mode shape rather than reduced total vibration energy. Survey multiple points, record actuator forces and support reactions where possible, and distinguish acceleration from velocity and displacement endpoints. Integrated response indices must be dimensionally explicit.

Before any structural interpretation, test the linear range, resonance behavior, and mounting repeatability. A small bench-scale result does not establish safe operation against large impacts.

X.4 Electromagnetic and optical carrier

The Photonic Φ-Lattice Engineering Release 1.0 supplies an example of controlled optical geometry and feasibility documentation, not an approved protective-field implementation. Its optical endpoints, forward models, and fabrication assumptions are confined to the optical study unless a new derivation demonstrates transferability.

For electromagnetic investigations, begin with Maxwell equations and the material constitutive relations. State whether the geometry is in a near-field, diffraction, scattering, or waveguide regime. Include detector response, polarization, source coherence, losses, and uncertainty in material properties.

An interference-visibility effect or a structured intensity pattern does not demonstrate an energy sink. The relevant flux is the Poynting vector under appropriate assumptions, and a complete energy interpretation requires accounting for absorption, scattering, transmission, and storage.

X.5 Thermal and high-energy exclusions

Thermal transport may be dominated by conduction, convection, radiation, and phase change rather than coherent wave interference. A laboratory demonstration of phase-sensitive acoustic or optical organization does not establish active heat rejection, thermal shielding, or protection from hazardous sources.

High-energy impact, ballistic, radiation, or human-exposure trials are outside the proposed first-stage laboratory envelope. No protective rating, safety claim, or operational deployment recommendation may be derived from the present manuscript.

X.6 Transferability decision rule

Classify each proposed cross-program transfer as direct, conditional, methodological, or prohibited. Direct transfer requires matching governing physics, measured quantity, calibration, geometry, and uncertainty. Conditional transfer requires an explicit new model and verification. Methodological transfer includes documentation controls, blind testing, and data provenance. Prohibited transfer includes reusing optical feasibility as evidence of human safety or mechanical protection.

For each claim, the reviewer shall record source artifact, source result, target claim, shared assumptions, changed assumptions, required new measurements, and approval status. The burden of proof increases as the application moves away from the original carrier and operating scale.

Appendix Y. Engineering Feasibility Trade Studies and Test-Article Selection

The following studies establish how a qualified laboratory could select a bounded, low-energy test article. They do not prescribe a final build, and all numerical limits remain subject to apparatus-specific safety review.

Y.1 Trade-study criteria and weighting

Compare candidate carriers against measurable signal-to-noise ratio, conventional-model tractability, calibration access, spatial resolution, source controllability, safety, equipment availability, fabrication complexity, and replication cost. Record the weight and scoring rationale before examining any favored design.

A weighted score can prioritize feasibility, but it does not replace hard safety gates. Any option failing independent shutdown, source monitoring, or essential calibration must be rejected regardless of its aggregate score. The initial study should optimize scientific interpretability rather than dramatic visual effect.

Y.2 Baseline apparatus block diagram

The proposed bench-scale architecture contains an independently monitored disturbance source, six individually addressable low-output actuators, a synchronized timing reference, isolated drive electronics, a spatial receiver array, a guard-region receiver set, environmental sensors, and an independent recorder. The control computer stores configuration commands and is separated from the hardware safety path.

The source monitor provides a reference for drift correction. The independent recorder acquires receiver data without relying on the same derived values used by the controller. A fault logger records overcurrent, overtemperature, communication loss, and timing invalidity. No controller state may override the hardware shutdown chain.

Y.3 Interface control: measurement and actuation

Each actuator channel requires an identifier, command range, physical-output calibration, voltage-current measurement, phase latency, thermal operating envelope, and fault state. Each receiver channel requires measurand, units, frequency response, uncertainty, physical location, and synchronization offset.

An interface change is considered material if it alters signal scaling, propagation delay, boundary loading, electrical power, or safety behavior. A change log shall identify which calibration and model datasets are invalidated.

Y.4 Timing budget allocation

For a phase-controlled trial, express the total relative phase uncertainty as contributions from clock skew, trigger jitter, digital scheduling, conversion delay, driver response, actuator response, propagation-path uncertainty, and estimation variance. Correlated terms must be handled through covariance rather than summed as independent root-mean-square errors.

Under the e^(+iωt) phasor convention, a positive pure delay Δt produces signed phase shift Δφ = −2πfΔt; its magnitude is 2πf|Δt|. This expression does not describe arbitrary frequency-dependent dispersive phase. The laboratory shall verify the approximation over the registered frequency band.

Y.5 Sensor grid and spatial aliasing

Grid design must account for the shortest wavelength, boundary-induced gradients, and expected null width. A sparse array can report an impressive local minimum while missing nearby amplification. A dense discovery grid is useful for mapping, but a separate held-out grid or independent session is needed for confirmatory inference.

Specify whether spatial aggregation weights are uniform, volume-based, or uncertainty-weighted. Do not change the weights after observing the active response. Report a map of signed changes and the spatial average alongside the most favorable point.

Y.6 Source normalization and power matching

Compare trial conditions using a continuously measured source reference. For active arrays, distinguish commanded amplitude, measured drive voltage, real electrical input power, and calibrated physical output. A constant digital command is not proof of constant delivered power.

The selected normalization should match the scientific question. A constant-input-power comparison tests allocation under equal electrical cost; a constant-physical-output comparison tests spatial patterning at equal delivered source strength. Report both when feasible and do not mix them within a single registered endpoint.

Y.7 Control architecture and bounded stability

The initial confirmatory phase study should favor frozen open-loop patterns because adaptive tuning can bias the endpoint. Closed-loop tests may follow with a separately registered objective, known plant delay, actuator saturation handling, and a stability review.

For a feedback plant P(s) and controller K(s), closed-loop sensitivity depends on the declared feedback sign convention; the familiar form S(s)=1/[1+P(s)K(s)] assumes negative feedback in the stated loop. Delay and model uncertainty can erode stability margin. No generic controller gain or universal 17-second lock may be asserted without measurement.

Y.8 Six-node geometric controls

The geometry matrix should include the nominal six-node arrangement, at least one control arrangement with comparable physical footprint, and a position-swapped condition. Node positions must be surveyed and documented, and any golden-ratio parameter must be treated as a candidate design variable rather than a proven privileged physical constant.

Pre-register which geometric contrast is primary. If many geometries or phase patterns are explored, correct the inference for selection or reserve an independent confirmation dataset. A geometry-dependent result that matches conventional interference predictions is still a valid engineering result.

Y.9 Mechanical and thermal parasitics

Mounting frames can couple actuators through elastic paths and create unintended resonances. Power electronics can generate heat, electromagnetic pickup, and audible or mechanical artifacts. Characterize these parasitic channels with actuators disabled, individually driven, and jointly driven.

Where feasible, add independent frame sensors and electrical pickup monitors. If the observed response changes when the support structure is modified, treat support coupling as a candidate conventional explanation before proposing an additional mechanism.

Y.10 Verification gate sequence

Gate A: approved low-energy modality, risk assessment, and instrument list. Gate B: calibrated source and independent acquisition. Gate C: individual-node transfer characterization. Gate D: all fifteen pairwise checks and conventional-model validation. Gate E: frozen full-array confirmatory comparison. Gate F: independent replication or documented feasibility disposition.

Each gate shall have an owner, dated evidence package, deviation log, and written decision. A gate is not passed merely because the next activity has begun.

Y.11 Failure modes and recovery evidence

Potential failures include loss of synchronization, source drift, sensor clipping, unstable feedback, unexpected node coupling, thermal derating, actuator misalignment, and false-positive selection of spatial nulls. Record the detection method and the safest response for each failure.

A failed run must remain in the trial inventory with an exclusion reason. Repeated failures may invalidate the test plan and require redesign. Do not silently substitute successful retests for failed attempts.

Y.12 Pilot cost and schedule methodology

Estimate cost by work package: engineering review, modelling, fabrication, calibration, test operations, analysis, independent replication, safety controls, and contingency. Use vendor quotes and laboratory rates rather than transferring an unrelated optical-study estimate into this application.

Schedule should show dependency gates and review time, not just fabrication time. The cost and schedule remain unestimated until a laboratory selects the modality, hardware, and required measurement precision.

Appendix AA. Reproducible Quantitative Example and Sensitivity Study

The numerical values in this appendix are synthetic examples for method verification. They are not results from a built CHRISTOS® protective apparatus, from the Photonic Φ-Lattice experiment, or from any independent laboratory. The objective is to make model assumptions, uncertainty calculations, and decision logic independently reproducible.

AA.1 Definition of a bounded linear test system

For a single frequency f, let y(f) be the vector of complex receiver amplitudes, d(f) the disturbance-only response, H(f) the receiver-by-actuator transfer matrix, and u(f) the vector of complex actuator inputs. The conventional linear prediction is y(f) = d(f) + H(f)u(f). Every entry requires a declared physical unit; if receiver amplitude is pressure in pascals and u is electrical voltage, H has units Pa/V. The matrix is frequency- and configuration-dependent.

For six nodes and m receivers, H is an m × 6 complex matrix. The model is a local approximation: it assumes time invariance over the acquisition interval, linearity within the bounded drive range, and a fixed environment. These assumptions must be tested before applying it to simultaneous excitation.

A receiver null is not a field-wide null. A model can achieve y approximately zero at one receiver while increasing the response at nearby locations. The measured output should therefore be evaluated over a registered spatial region and a separate guard region.

AA.2 A two-receiver numerical example

Consider a deliberately simple, dimensionless two-receiver case. The disturbance response is d = [1, 1] and a single actuator produces H = [1, −0.5]. Choosing actuator command u = −1 gives y = [0, 1.5]. The first receiver shows perfect cancellation, but the second receiver rises from 1 to 1.5 in amplitude.

For an equally weighted squared-amplitude index, the baseline is 1² + 1² = 2. The active index is 0² + 1.5² = 2.25, an increase of 12.5 percent. The example demonstrates why local cancellation alone cannot establish net attenuation, energy absorption, or a return pathway.

If a researcher reported only the first receiver, the result would misleadingly appear to be a 100 percent reduction. This calculation is not an energy balance because the dimensionless receiver amplitudes are not integrated energy flux measurements.

AA.3 Regularized multi-receiver optimization

For a fixed frequency, define a nonnegative diagonal spatial weighting matrix W and a positive semidefinite input penalty R. One conventional objective is J(u) = (d + Hu)ᴴ W (d + Hu) + λ uᴴ R u, where λ ≥ 0 is a declared regularization parameter and superscript H denotes conjugate transpose.

If the unconstrained quadratic problem is well posed, the stationary solution is u* = −(HᴴWH + λR)⁻¹HᴴWd. The inverse exists only when the penalized normal matrix is nonsingular. In other cases, use a documented pseudoinverse or a constrained numerical optimizer. The result is a conventional control prediction, not a Christfield-specific mechanism.

Physical actuator limits require additional constraints, such as |uᵢ| ≤ uᵢ,max and measured real electrical power within a safe bound. A complex command magnitude does not directly determine real power when impedance and phase vary with frequency. The optimization shall be frozen before held-out evaluation.

AA.4 Sensitivity to calibration errors

Let the estimated transfer matrix be Ĥ = H + ΔH and the implemented command be û = u + Δu. To first order, the receiver prediction error is Δy ≈ ΔH u + H Δu + Δd, where Δd denotes disturbance drift. This relation identifies three ordinary sources of residuals that can mimic a new physical effect.

For each matrix element, measure amplitude and phase repeatability over separate calibration sessions. Use a perturbation study to propagate plausible calibration variation through the frozen optimizer. Record the worst-case and distribution-based change in the registered endpoint.

A candidate residual is not interpretable until it exceeds the combined measurement and model uncertainty in a way that is stable across sessions and insensitive to reasonable conventional model variants.

AA.5 Timing-to-phase conversion example

For a pure time delay, the phase-error magnitude is |Δφ| = 2πf|Δt|. At f = 1,000 Hz and |Δt| = 10 microseconds, |Δφ| = 0.06283 radians, or approximately 3.6 degrees. At 10,000 Hz, the magnitude is approximately 36 degrees. Under the e^(+iωt) convention, a positive delay produces a negative signed phase shift.

The conversion illustrates why a timing specification must be derived from the highest registered frequency. It does not capture dispersive actuator response, frequency-dependent group delay, phase-wrap ambiguity, or propagation-path changes. Those effects require measured transfer functions.

When timing uncertainty is correlated between channels, naive root-sum-square calculations can understate or overstate the phase budget. Include covariance terms or empirically estimate the distribution of relative phase error.

AA.6 Measurement noise and minimum resolvable effect

Suppose the registered difference between active and sham conditions is Δ and the standard uncertainty of the difference is u(Δ). A useful signal-to-uncertainty diagnostic is z = Δ/u(Δ), but a large z is not sufficient if the analysis ignored trial clustering, model selection, or repeated endpoint searches.

Independent experimental units are separate randomized runs or sessions as defined in the protocol, not individual digitizer samples. If 100,000 samples are collected during one continuously tuned run, that does not create 100,000 independent demonstrations.

The feasibility team shall select the minimum effect of interest based on engineering relevance and sensor capability, then estimate the required number of independent units using pilot variance. No sample count is asserted in this manuscript without such information.

AA.7 Power normalization and conversion factors

For an electrical channel with measured voltage v(t) and current i(t), average real power over a registered window T is P = (1/T)∫₀ᵀ v(t)i(t)dt. RMS voltage multiplied by RMS current is apparent power and equals real power only under the relevant phase and waveform conditions.

For an acoustic example, a pressure-squared index has units Pa² and cannot be reported as joules without a justified intensity relation, area, duration, and propagation assumptions. For mechanical vibration, acceleration-squared likewise is not mechanical energy without an appropriate mass, velocity, and boundary model.

Comparisons shall report both the endpoint and the actual electrical power delivered to each active node. This prevents a fixed command level from hiding changes in loading or source output.

AA.8 Model residuals and interpretation

Define a signed residual r = ymeasured − ypredicted at each registered receiver and frequency. Report both real and imaginary components, or equivalent amplitude and phase quantities with consistent conventions. A scalar residual norm should not conceal coherent spatial structure.

Before interpreting r, test alternate plausible boundary impedances, support couplings, source drift, sensor delay, and actuator nonlinearity. Model revision after viewing confirmatory results converts that dataset into exploratory evidence for the revised model.

A Christfield-specific interpretation would require a separate quantitative model that predicts a measurable effect not already captured by conventional equations. The inward return-flow hypothesis is preserved, but an unmodelled residual alone is not evidence that the hypothesis is correct.

AA.9 Independent numerical verification package

The computational archive shall contain the source matrix and receiver geometry, frequency grid, calibrated H values, disturbance vector d, spatial weights W, regularization λ, constraints, solver tolerances, and the resulting predicted outputs. Include software versions, random seeds where used, and an executable or plainly specified calculation procedure.

For each candidate design, compare a direct matrix prediction with an independently implemented calculation. For any higher-fidelity simulation, document mesh or discretization convergence, material parameters, boundary conditions, and solver residuals. Agreement between two scripts using identical incorrect assumptions is not independent physical validation.

The archive shall also preserve negative or inconclusive configurations, because selectively publishing only the strongest numerical cancellation can misrepresent robustness.

Appendix AB. Design Freeze, Requirements Allocation, and Laboratory Decision Records

AB.1 Requirements hierarchy and acceptance evidence

System requirements shall begin with an explicitly benign measurable objective. Each requirement must identify its rationale, owning subsystem, verification method, acceptance threshold, and evidence location. Avoid requirements such as “creates a force field” that cannot be verified without an operational physical definition.

A requirement for spatial response reduction shall specify the measured carrier, target volume or grid, frequency band, baseline condition, allowable source drift, uncertainty, and guard-region behavior. An equipment requirement shall specify a rated operating envelope and a method for verifying it.

Assign a status of proposed, allocated, verified, waived, or failed. A proposed requirement is not a demonstrated capability. Waivers require dated justification and cannot waive independent safety controls.

AB.2 Six-node interface allocation

Each node shall have a unique identifier, coordinate position, orientation, mechanical attachment, physical output calibration, electrical power monitor, timing reference, command channel, and fault indication. The interface-control drawing shall show all signal and energy connections as well as isolation boundaries.

Centralized control may simplify clock distribution but introduces common-mode failure. Distributed control can improve modularity while requiring measured synchronization and network-latency behavior. The laboratory should select the architecture after reviewing its timing and fault requirements.

The interface matrix must distinguish measured physical output from commanded input. A controller setpoint is not a measurement of delivered force, pressure, field strength, or power.

AB.3 Calibration and configuration freeze

Freeze the apparatus geometry, sensor positions, channel mapping, firmware revision, and analysis configuration before confirmatory trials. Every freeze record shall include the current source file identifiers and a list of open deviations.

Calibration shall cover sensor amplitude response, channel phase, timing skew, actuator transfer function, and source monitoring under representative conditions. Record both as-found and as-left results when instruments are adjusted.

Any calibration performed after observing confirmatory outcomes must be reported transparently. If a material change affects the forward model or endpoint, repeat the relevant confirmatory trial under a new configuration identifier.

AB.4 Safety-critical control boundaries

Safety shutdown shall not depend solely on the primary research controller, and fault responses shall be tested under deliberately simulated nonhazardous failures. Relevant faults include lost clock reference, stuck output, sensor dropout, communication timeout, overheating, and power interruption.

The risk assessment must document mechanical restraint, electrical protection, thermal limits, operator exclusion zones where needed, and an independent stop mechanism. Any later work involving human exposure or hazardous environments requires a separate safety case and approvals.

No protective demonstration should expose people, property, or critical equipment to a hazard on the assumption that an unverified mechanism will function.

AB.5 Evidence maturity and release decisions

Use separate decisions for design feasibility, safe apparatus commissioning, measured response reproducibility, model adequacy, and any broader physical interpretation. These are not interchangeable approvals.

A laboratory feasibility decision may approve further design work without authorizing fabrication. A commissioning decision may authorize bounded tests without implying that an effect has been demonstrated. A reproducible result may still be fully explained by conventional wave physics.

The review record shall name the decision-maker, date, applicable configuration, evidence considered, unresolved limitations, and next authorized activity.

Appendix AD. Reference Test Case and Reproducible Numerical Verification

This appendix specifies a fully synthetic, low-amplitude scalar-wave benchmark for software verification. It is not an experimental result, a fabrication instruction, or evidence of a non-barrier protective effect. All quantities are explicitly illustrative. Its purpose is to establish a reproducible calculation with known outputs and falsifiable numerical checks.

AD.1 Model domain and sign conventions

Assume time dependence exp(+iωt) and a single harmonic frequency. The complex receiver response is y = d + H u + ε, where d is the disturbance-only receiver vector, H is the measured or simulated complex transfer matrix, u is the six-component actuator input vector, and ε is measurement noise. With this convention, a propagation delay τ contributes exp(−iωτ). The real-valued measured waveform is Re[y exp(+iωt)].

The model is linear only within its declared amplitude range. A receiver vector can represent pressure, velocity, displacement, or another coherent signal, but every component of a given model must share compatible units. Matrix columns have receiver-unit per actuator-input-unit. An electromagnetic interpretation requires a separate Maxwell model and cannot be inferred from this scalar benchmark.

AD.2 Synthetic six-node geometry

Define six candidate node centers on a horizontal circle: ri = (R cos θi, R sin θi, 0), with θi = 2π(i−1)/6 for i = 1,…,6, where R is the node-circle radius. Place the disturbance source and the nominal target receiver on the x-axis on opposite sides of the node circle. These definitions describe a numerical illustration, not a preferred physical design. Specific radii and coordinates are not disclosed in this public version.

A second, held-out receiver grid is required to test generalization. Define a 5 × 5 grid of 25 positions spanning the target region. Do not use held-out receiver values to optimize the phase pattern. Include an exterior guard grid beyond the target region to detect displacement of response maxima.

AD.3 Transfer kernel and its limits

For a synthetic homogeneous scalar medium, define G(r,s;f) = exp(−ik|r−s|)/(4π|r−s|), k = 2πf/c. This is a free-space Green-function kernel for a point source with the stated time convention. The 1/r singularity means the point-source model is invalid at the source location. It also ignores boundaries, near-field transducer structure, supports, and material loss.

Let Hji = ai G(rj,ri;f), where ai is a declared complex actuator calibration factor with units selected to make Hji dimensionally consistent. Let dj = ad G(rj,rd;f). Do not choose calibration factors by fitting held-out measurements. For a room or test fixture, replace the free-space kernel with a boundary-aware model before comparing to physical data.

AD.4 Weighted objective and control limits

For target receiver weights wj ≥ 0, define Jtarget(u) = Σj wj |dj + (Hu)_j|². Define a guard penalty Jguard(u) = Σg vg max(0, |dg+(Hg u)_g|² − bg)², with bg a predeclared guard threshold in squared receiver units. The squared penalty changes the units of the objective and therefore requires a properly scaled multiplier.

The illustrative optimization minimizes Jtarget + λ Jguard + μ||u||² subject to |ui| ≤ umax,i and Σi Pi(ui) ≤ Pmax. Electrical input power Pi must be measured or characterized; it is not automatically proportional to |ui|² for a nonlinear or reactive drive. This mathematical objective is not an energy-conservation equation.

AD.5 Unconstrained solution and numerical conditioning

If λ = 0 and power is represented by a quadratic regularizer, the unconstrained complex least-squares solution satisfies (HᴴWH + μI)u = −HᴴWd. Here Hᴴ denotes the conjugate transpose and W is a nonnegative diagonal receiver-weight matrix. The regularization coefficient μ must carry units that make both terms compatible.

Use a stable linear solver or singular-value decomposition rather than explicitly forming a matrix inverse. Record the singular values, effective condition number, residual norm, regularization choice, and floating-point precision. If H is rank-deficient, infinitely many controls may produce the same fitted response; a minimum-norm solution is not proof of a unique physical mechanism.

AD.6 Phase-only and amplitude-limited variants

For phase-only control, write ui = ai exp(iφi) with ai fixed and φi bounded to [−π,π). This is a nonlinear optimization; the unconstrained least-squares expression does not solve it. Compare a documented multi-start search against a fixed-phase control and random phase assignments under the same total measured input.

For amplitude-limited control, include actuator saturation, drive-to-output phase lag, and changes in effective transfer function under simultaneous excitation. The selected phase pattern must be frozen before evaluating held-out receiver positions.

AD.7 Source normalization and drift

Let st be an independent complex or amplitude-only source monitor during trial t. If the source-to-monitor relationship is stable and linear, normalize receiver response by a registered function of st. An amplitude-only monitor cannot correct unknown source phase drift; that limitation must be included in the uncertainty model.

Use repeated disturbance-only measurements to detect baseline changes over time. Randomization can reduce systematic allocation bias, but it cannot substitute for measuring source drift.

AD.8 Numerical convergence protocol

Evaluate the same geometry at progressively refined receiver grids and, where a numerical PDE solver is used, refined spatial meshes. Define the convergence observable before calculation: for example, target-region integrated squared response, guard-region maximum, and phase at specified reference points.

Report the absolute and relative change between successive resolutions, and assess whether the change is small compared with the intended experimental uncertainty. Convergence of a simplified model does not establish that its boundary conditions represent the laboratory.

AD.9 Perturbation and identifiability analysis

Perturb node positions, orientation, gain, phase delay, source location, and receiver calibration within independently justified uncertainty bounds. Propagate each perturbation through the forward model and quantify its effect on the primary endpoint. Report the largest contributors and any strongly correlated parameters.

A model with more adjustable parameters than independent observations may reproduce nearly any response. Use singular values or a profile-likelihood analysis to identify weakly constrained combinations, and collect additional measurements rather than interpreting unstable fitted parameters as new physics.

AD.10 Required reproducibility artifacts

Archive a geometry file with units and coordinate system, a model assumptions sheet, the transfer matrix, disturbance vector, optimization constraints, random seed, software version, solver tolerances, and a script producing target and guard response maps. Include a machine-readable table of the synthetic benchmark outputs.

The reproducibility test passes only if a second analyst obtains the declared values within numerical tolerances using the same assumptions. A numerical pass validates the implementation of that model, not the underlying Christfield hypothesis.

AD.11 Worked cancellation example and energy caution

Consider a single receiver with normalized disturbance d = 1 + 0i and combined actuator response Hu = −0.8 + 0i. The receiver amplitude changes from 1.0 to 0.2; the squared-amplitude index changes from 1.00 to 0.04, a 96% reduction at that receiver. Expressed as a ratio, 10 log10(1.00/0.04) ≈ 13.98 dB. This calculation does not establish a 96% reduction in total energy, because the index is local and the actuator supplies additional power.

At another receiver, d = 1 + 0i and Hu = +0.8 + 0i produce amplitude 1.8 and squared-amplitude index 3.24. The same active arrangement can therefore suppress one receiver and amplify another. A full experiment must measure spatial redistribution and energy flow rather than relying on a single favorable point.

Appendix AE. Christfield Dynamics Interface Specification and Hypothesis Controls

This appendix integrates the current conceptual and engineering maturity of the separate Christfield Dynamics program into the non-barrier manuscript without importing unverified physical conclusions. The originating proposal concerns possible organization through an inward return pathway toward a singularity-like center. It does not require a literal black hole, a mandatory toroidal geometry, or an additional force. The Photonic Φ-Lattice is an experimental implementation and not the hypothesis itself.

AE.1 Levels of evidence and permitted transfer

Level A is an originating conceptual hypothesis. Level B is a mathematical candidate with defined variables and assumptions. Level C is a computational result within a specified conventional or alternative model. Level D is an independently measured laboratory effect. Level E is a prospective result discriminating between explanatory models. The optical engineering feasibility release concerns readiness for laboratory assessment and should not be relabelled Level D or E.

Transfer from optical geometry to non-barrier engineering is valid only for traceable methodological elements such as controlled configurations, calibration discipline, forward modelling, and evidence review. Optical coherence, acoustic cancellation, mechanical attenuation, and electromagnetic shielding require their own physical models and observations.

AE.2 Operationalization of an additional pathway

An additional return-flow model would require a physical state variable q with declared units, a governing evolution equation, a coupling operator to a measurable conventional field, initial and boundary conditions, and a unique predicted observable. The symbol q here is a placeholder, not an established Christfield quantity.

For example, a candidate extension of the conventional model may be written y = d + Hu + Kq + ε, but K and q cannot both be arbitrary fitted functions. Their dimensions, allowed parameters, causal structure, and prior predictions must be fixed before confirmatory testing. If Kq is observationally equivalent to an unmeasured conventional source or boundary effect, the model is not identifiable.

AE.3 Energy accounting requirement for a proposed return channel

If a return pathway is claimed to transport energy, specify a flux vector Jreturn in W/m², its boundary surface and outward normal, and its relationship to measured field quantities. The integral ∮Jreturn·n dA has units of watts. A postulated scalar coherence index cannot be substituted for this flux without a derived conversion law.

Any proposed return term must be evaluated against measured actuator electrical input, source input, stored energy, conventional fluxes, dissipation, and support reactions. An unexplained closure residual is an unresolved measurement or modelling problem until independent evidence excludes ordinary missing terms.

AE.4 Phase and coherence language

Phase coherence can be defined from cross-spectral measurements or an explicitly defined order parameter. It is a statistical or wave-kinematic quantity, not automatically a new physical field. The mathematical object used to describe coherence must have a stated domain, normalization, units, and measurement procedure.

The foundational Christfield expression X = δC/δΨ remains an open formalization question unless C, Ψ, the functional derivative, and units are operationally specified. The non-barrier manuscript shall cite it as an unresolved candidate rather than use it to calculate protective performance.

AE.5 Prospective prediction gate

Before assigning a Christfield-specific interpretation, the alternative model must forecast an observable not already adjustable within the conventional model class, with a stated sign, magnitude, frequency or spatial dependence, and uncertainty. The model and parameters must be frozen before the confirmatory dataset is collected.

A failure to fit a simple linear transfer matrix is not sufficient. Relevant conventional alternatives include nonlinear actuator response, support coupling, material anisotropy, multipath propagation, sensor loading, control latency, and thermal drift. A positive result should be reported at the narrowest level it supports.

AE.6 Relationship to the three proposed Christfield experiments

The optical experiment examines geometry and optical response. The human-intention experiment, if pursued, requires its own safeguards, controls, endpoints, and independent review. A prospective combined experiment cannot inherit confirmation from either component.

The proposed non-barrier system constitutes another application-specific investigation. Its scientific status is not upgraded simply because an optical feasibility package was accepted for laboratory consideration.

Appendix AG. Integrated Engineering Calculation Dossier

This dossier translates the research-stage non-barrier concept into traceable engineering calculations. All values in the examples are synthetic and illustrative. No value represents a measurement of a constructed CHRISTOS® protective system. Calculations are framed for a benign, bounded physical disturbance selected by a qualified laboratory.

AG.1 Configuration baseline and reproducibility boundary

The calculation baseline shall identify the disturbance carrier, material properties, operating band, test article, source, node coordinates, receiving sensors, mounting supports, and surrounding boundaries. A numerical result without this configuration is not a portable engineering specification. A version change to any relevant component requires a new calculation applicability review.

The six-node arrangement is a candidate experimental architecture. It is not a required geometry of the originating inward return-flow hypothesis. The same scientific question could be investigated using alternative topologies if the physical carrier, governing model, and measurements are specified.

Each calculation receives an identifier, revision, responsible analyst, input-file hash, assumptions, software version, unit checks, independent check status, and corresponding verification requirement. Use the configuration identifier consistently across drawings, source code, data files, and laboratory notebooks.

AG.2 Complex transfer model and signal conventions

For a linear time-invariant approximation at angular frequency ω, write y(ω)=d(ω)+H(ω)u(ω)+ε(ω), where y and d are m-component measured complex response vectors, u is the six-component actuator input vector, H is an m-by-six transfer matrix, and ε collects measurement and model error. This is a conventional superposition model, not a Christfield-specific law.

Each Hji has units of receiver quantity divided by actuator input quantity. For example, pressure per volt has units Pa/V, whereas displacement per newton has units m/N. Mixing input conventions across nodes makes the fitted matrix physically uninterpretable. Define phasor amplitude as RMS or peak and use one convention throughout.

At a given frequency, H should be estimated from independently excited node trials under stable conditions. A multi-input fit may be useful, but its rank, conditioning, and cross-talk must be documented. The fitted H does not provide evidence of a nonconventional return channel merely because a selected u produces cancellation.

AG.3 Solvability and rank of the six-node system

The number of actuators does not by itself guarantee control of the target region. For m receivers, the rank of H determines how many independent complex response directions are available. If the target disturbance has components outside the column space of H, exact cancellation is impossible under the linear model.

Use singular-value decomposition H=UΣV*, with singular values σk. Small singular values indicate poorly controlled directions and high sensitivity to calibration error. Report the singular-value spectrum over the frequency band, not only a single condition number. If the effective rank varies with frequency, the attainable spatial response changes with frequency.

For an illustrative normalized 3-by-6 transfer matrix with singular values 2.0, 0.8, and 0.04, the nonzero-singular-value condition number is 50. A target component aligned with the weakest direction requires much larger actuator effort and is particularly sensitive to errors. These numbers are an example of interpretation, not a measured CHRISTOS® matrix.

AG.4 Regularized constrained control solution

An illustrative optimization objective is J(u)=||W(d+Hu)||²+λ||Lu||², subject to bounds on input amplitude, delivered power, and allowable response in a guard region. W weights target-region error; L penalizes actuator effort or a selected control mode; λ is a nonnegative regularization weight.

When bounds are absent and the matrices are compatible, the stationary solution is u*=−(H*W*WH+λL*L)^(−1)H*W*Wd if the inverse exists. In practical implementation, solve the linear system or use a stable factorization rather than explicitly forming an inverse. This expression does not guarantee stable closed-loop operation.

Choosing λ by minimizing error on the same data used to estimate H is an optimistic procedure. Set λ using a separate calibration or validation set, freeze it, and report confirmatory results without retuning. Guard-region constraints and real electrical input limits may change the optimum substantially.

AG.5 Spatial objective and adverse redistribution

A target-point reduction may be real while the total disturbance field is merely redistributed. Define a target-region statistic using an explicit spatial measure and a guard-region statistic on an adjacent or surrounding region. If the carrier is acoustic pressure, a spatial average of squared pressure is a pressure-squared index; it becomes acoustic intensity only under additional assumptions about particle velocity or wave impedance.

For a simple illustration, a target sensor drops from 1.0 to 0.5 units of amplitude, while a guard sensor rises from 1.0 to 1.4 units. Their squared indices change from 1.00 to 0.25 and from 1.00 to 1.96, respectively. The target reduction is 75% in squared amplitude, while the guard increase is 96%. Neither percentage establishes net energy change.

Use spatially representative measurements and account for boundaries before making statements about global attenuation. The manuscript uses non-barrier as a design concept, not as a demonstrated ability to protect people or structures.

AG.6 Phase delay, synchronization, and bandwidth

For a pure delay τ, the phase contribution at frequency f is φ=−2πfτ under the e^(+iωt) phasor convention. A timing uncertainty σt produces an approximate phase uncertainty σ_φ≈2πfσt when the delay model applies. At 1 kHz, 10 microseconds correspond to approximately 3.6 degrees.

Total phase uncertainty also includes analog filtering, actuator phase response, sensor group delay, source propagation, timestamp alignment, and temperature-dependent changes. A common digital clock reduces some timing errors but does not calibrate the full analog signal chain.

Wideband signals cannot generally be controlled by assigning one constant phase angle to each node. Group delay, frequency-dependent H, and actuator bandwidth must be considered. A narrowband proof of concept does not establish broadband protection.

AG.7 Sampling, aliasing, and spectral leakage

For the selected measurement band, the sample rate must exceed twice the highest relevant signal frequency, with additional margin for practical antialias filtering and instrument response. An actuator may produce out-of-band components even if the command is narrowband. These components can alias into the measurement band without suitable analog filtering.

Frequency-domain transfer estimates require a declared window, record duration, overlap, estimator, and confidence procedure. Spectral leakage can masquerade as a small residual if source and actuator frequencies drift relative to analysis windows. Preserve raw waveforms so that an independent reviewer can recompute the spectra.

For a 2-second acquisition, the nominal Fourier-bin spacing is 0.5 Hz. Zero-padding can interpolate the display but does not increase independent spectral resolution. This example illustrates a processing property, not a recommended acquisition duration.

AG.8 Closed-loop stability and passivity challenge

If a controller K(s) acts on a plant G(s) under conventional negative feedback, define the loop transfer L(s)=G(s)K(s) and sensitivity S(s)=1/(1+L(s)) only after specifying the sign convention and feedback topology. Multi-input systems require matrix sensitivity and an analysis of poles or appropriate robustness metrics.

Latency, actuator saturation, sensor noise, and model error can destabilize a controller that appears effective in an ideal frequency-domain calculation. The laboratory should first characterize bounded open-loop response, then commission closed-loop behavior under independent hardware safety limits.

Where a passivity or energy-based safety argument is used, its storage function, input-output supply rate, and domain of validity must be derived. A generic claim that the system redirects energy toward ground does not replace a measured mechanical reaction or electrical and thermal power ledger.

AG.9 Real power and apparent power

For a periodic electrical drive, real average power is the time average of v(t)i(t), with sign and measurement point declared. For sinusoidal RMS voltage V and current I with phase difference θ, P=VI cos θ. The product VI alone is apparent power, not necessarily dissipated or delivered real power.

For an illustrative actuator, the real average power is the product of rms voltage, rms current, and power factor. If six identical and independent channels operated together, their combined real input would be six times the single-channel value. Actual multi-node operation must be measured rather than inferred from this simplification. Specific numerical values are not disclosed in this public version.

Measure voltage and current at a defined electrical boundary with bandwidth sufficient for harmonics. Report amplifier loss and power-supply overhead separately if the control-volume boundary includes them.

AG.10 Thermal storage and dissipation

For a component approximated as a lumped thermal mass, Cth dT/dt=Ploss−(T−Tamb)/Rth, where Cth is J/K and Rth is K/W. This model is appropriate only when internal temperature gradients and boundary variations are negligible over the relevant time scale.

An illustrative component with thermal resistance Rth dissipating power P has a predicted steady temperature rise of Rth P in the lumped linear model. That relationship does not establish safety: component ratings, local hotspots, contact temperature, ventilation, and fault conditions must be assessed. Specific numerical values are not disclosed in this public version.

Any proposed transfer of energy into a return pathway requires evidence beyond a missing temperature rise. Unmeasured mechanical work, radiation, stored energy, boundary flux, and measurement error remain conventional alternatives.

AG.11 Mechanical momentum and support reactions

If the disturbance carrier exerts forces, the momentum balance must include support reactions and momentum flux through the selected control volume. Reducing vibration at one sensor can shift forces to a mount or adjacent structure. An isolated displacement trace cannot establish that the impulse has disappeared.

For a rigid support and measured force F(t), the impulse over [t0,t1] is I=∫F(t)dt, in newton-seconds. A constant illustrative force of 0.2 N over 0.1 s corresponds to 0.02 N·s. In a real test, force bandwidth, sensor dynamics, and multiple reaction paths matter.

Where support forces cannot be instrumented, report the momentum ledger as open. No protective-impact claim is permitted from a low-amplitude wave-control experiment.

AG.12 Energy control volume and closure residual

Define EV as energy stored inside a specified physical control volume. With outward energy flux J·n, supplied power Pin, and internal conversion to heat counted as an internal transfer rather than a boundary loss, the total-energy balance may be written dEV/dt+∮J·n dA=Pin. If thermal energy is excluded from EV, a separately defined heat-conversion term is required; avoid double counting.

An experimental closure residual is the difference between measured inputs and measured storage changes plus outward transfers, under one explicit convention. Propagate uncertainties and identify unmeasured pathways. A statistically significant closure residual indicates incomplete closure, not by itself an unconventional physical channel.

For a Christfield-specific return-flow model to enter the ledger, it must identify a carrier or measurable coupling, a flux with units of W/m² or an equivalent integrated power in W, a boundary or receiver, and a prospectively predicted change under a controlled manipulation.

AG.13 Uncertainty propagation and correlated errors

For an endpoint g(z) of measured inputs z with covariance matrix Σz, the first-order variance approximation is Var(g)≈∇gᵀΣz∇g. Correlations matter when several sensors share a clock, calibration reference, mounting structure, or source monitor. Treating correlated channels as independent can substantially understate uncertainty.

For a ratio R=A/B with positive B, uncertainty is particularly sensitive to uncertainty in the denominator when B is small. If the source monitor approaches its noise floor, a normalized response may become unstable. Specify denominator acceptance criteria and report the original unnormalized signals.

Use simulation or bootstrap methods where linear propagation is inadequate, while preserving the actual trial and session hierarchy. More digitized samples do not create more independent experiments.

AG.14 Synthetic model verification and blind recovery

Before laboratory trials, generate synthetic datasets with known H, source drift, correlated noise, phase offsets, and missing samples. Verify that the analysis pipeline recovers the planted parameters within prespecified numerical tolerance and flags corrupted or excluded records correctly.

Include adversarial cases where a spurious apparent reduction is created by source drift, calibration mismatch, and an unmodelled passive boundary. The pipeline should either identify these cases or report that the result cannot be interpreted. A successful synthetic recovery validates software behavior only, not the proposed physical mechanism.

Archive the synthetic generator separately from the confirmatory analysis, together with the random seed, software environment, and expected outputs. An independent analyst should reproduce the calculation without editing the test data.

AG.16 Quantitative decision gates

Gate D0 requires a complete configuration, safe modality selection, instrument list, calibration plan, and conventional forward model. Gate D1 requires demonstrated sensor sensitivity and a bounded, independently monitored apparatus. Gate D2 requires a reproducible conventional model fit to independent control data.

Gate D3 permits a confirmatory comparison only after the endpoint, effect threshold, sample-size justification, randomization, exclusions, and model parameters are frozen. Gate D4 requires independent reproduction and complete accounting of observed spatial and power redistribution. Gate D5 considers a Christfield-specific interpretation only if a prespecified alternative makes a unique prediction and survives adversarial conventional tests.

Failure at a gate triggers revision, not retrospective reclassification of the observation. The project may still produce publishable conventional engineering knowledge even if no additional mechanism is supported.
